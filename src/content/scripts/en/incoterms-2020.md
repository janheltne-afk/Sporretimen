*Length: allow 15–18 minutes. This is denser material than the container episode,
so take pauses between the sections.*

*Important for this one: do not read out all eleven rules. The table stays up as
a graphic while you explain the system. Anyone who has understood the staircase
can place a rule they have never heard of.*

## The opening

*(Straight in.)*

Three letters in a contract.

They decide who pays when the container is left standing at the terminal. Who takes
the loss when the goods arrive damaged. And who has to deal with the customs
authorities in a country they have never set foot in.

Three letters. And most of the people writing them into a contract could not explain
what they mean.

Today we are going to make them make sense.

## What the rules do not do

*(Graphic: the scope – what the rules allocate versus what they say nothing about.
Leave it up for a while. This is the most important part of the episode.)*

And we begin with what people most often overlook: what the rules do **not** do.

Incoterms allocate four things. Who does what – transport, customs clearance,
documents. Who pays for what. Where the risk for the goods passes from seller to
buyer. And who, if anyone, has to insure.

That is all.

The rules say **nothing** about when title passes. Nothing about price, payment or
payment terms. Nothing about what happens in the event of breach or delay. And
nothing about which country's law applies, or where a dispute is to be settled.

*(Say this sentence slowly. It clears up most of the misunderstandings.)*

Incoterms are not a contract of sale. They are a building block in one.

Risk passing in Rotterdam does not mean the buyer owns the goods in Rotterdam.
Title is governed by the contract and by the law that applies to it. A lot of
disputes start right here – because someone assumed three letters decided more than
they do.

## Two families

*(Graphic: the two groups, seven and four.)*

The rules are split into two groups, and that split matters more than most people
think.

**Seven of them apply to any mode of transport** – road, rail, air, sea, or several
in combination. Those are EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU and DDP.

**Four of them apply only to sea and inland waterway transport.** Those are FAS, FOB,
CFR and CIF.

Those four were written for goods loaded over the rail or placed alongside the ship –
bulk, break bulk, project cargo.

*(Pause. This sets up the container point later.)*

Not containers. Make a note of that, because we are coming back to it.

## The staircase from E to D

*(Graphic: the table with all eleven. Leave it up through this whole section –
you point, you do not read it out.)*

Now to the system. Because the first letter is not arbitrary. It tells you how much
of the job the seller has taken on.

**E** is EXW. The seller makes the goods available at their own premises, and does no
more. The buyer carries the most.

**F** is FCA, FAS and FOB. The seller delivers to the carrier the buyer has arranged.

**C** is CPT, CIP, CFR and CIF. The seller pays the freight onward – but does not carry
the risk that far.

**D** is DAP, DPU and DDP. The seller carries the risk all the way to the arrival point.

*(Here is the point. Build up to it.)*

Four steps, from least to most seller responsibility. And once you have the four, you
can place a rule you have never seen before.

But the C group catches people out. It looks like a delivered term, because the seller
pays the freight to the destination.

The risk left the seller at departure.

## The two points

*(Graphic: CIF Rotterdam, cost point versus risk point, side by side. This is the
core of the episode – take your time.)*

And this is the single misunderstanding that costs the most money.

Take CIF Rotterdam.

**The cost point is Rotterdam.** The seller pays freight and insurance there. That is
what is in the contract, and that is what people remember.

**The risk point is the port of departure.** Risk passed when the goods were on board.
If something goes wrong on the way, it is the buyer's loss. The buyer has to claim on
the insurance – not from the seller.

*(Pause.)*

Two places. One rule.

In the E, F and D rules, the two coincide. In the C rules, they never do.

If you take one thing away from this episode, make it that.

## The container mistake

*(Graphic: the rule, with FOB struck through and FCA beside it.)*

Now to the most common mistake in practice. It is easy to avoid once you know about it.

If the goods travel in a container, FOB is almost always the wrong rule. Use FCA.

Why? FOB moves the risk when the goods are on board the ship. But a container is handed
over at a terminal – often days before it is loaded. The seller loses control the moment
it is delivered there.

With FOB, the seller nonetheless carries the risk for a container they can neither see
nor touch.

FCA moves the risk to where the handover actually happens.

The same goes in pairs: where someone has written CFR and CIF, it should say CPT and CIP.

*(Make clear this is not nitpicking.)*

And this is not a technicality. The combination of "FOB" and "container" appears in a
great many contracts, and ICC has pointed out across several editions of the rules that
it is a misuse.

## What changed in 2020

*(Graphic: the 2010 → 2020 table. You do not need to cover all six – take the first
three, and say the rest is in the article.)*

The changes from 2010 to 2020 are not dramatic, but some of them have practical
consequences.

**DAT became DPU.** The name now says the decisive thing: delivered unloaded. And the rule
is no longer tied to a terminal – the place can be anywhere.

**CIP now requires full cover.** The seller has to insure at the highest standard level.
CIF stayed at minimum cover. So the two are no longer the same on that point, and that is
worth knowing.

**FCA and the on-board bill of lading.** The parties can agree that the buyer instructs the
carrier to issue an on-board bill of lading to the seller. That solves an old problem for
anyone who has to present documents under a letter of credit.

*(Important close to this section.)*

And one thing that surprises people: older versions do not stop applying. If the parties
agree "Incoterms 2010", that is what governs between them.

Which is exactly why the year has to be in the contract.

## Three traps

*(No graphic needed here – or three short cards, one per trap.)*

Three traps before we wrap up.

**The first: DDP is easier to promise than to deliver.** The seller takes on import
clearance and all duties in the buyer's country. But in many countries a foreign seller
cannot act as importer without registering, and VAT cannot always be recovered. What was
meant as good service becomes a cost nobody had calculated.

**The second: EXW sits badly across borders.** The seller has no obligation to clear the
goods for export or to load them. So the buyer has to arrange export from a country they
may have no presence in, and needs documentation only the seller holds. ICC itself points
out that the rule is best suited to domestic trade. If you want something similar, FCA is
usually the better fit.

**The third: insurance is minimum unless otherwise agreed.** Under CIF the seller's duty is
minimum cover, and it covers less than most people assume. If the buyer wants more, it has
to be agreed separately.

## How to write it properly

*(Graphic: the three parts, numbered.)*

And now the practical part. A delivery term missing one of three parts is a dispute waiting
to happen.

**One: the rule.** The three letters. Pick the one that matches the mode of transport – not
the one you have always used.

**Two: the place, as precisely as possible.** Not just "Oslo", but which terminal, which
gate, which address. That is where the risk changes hands.

**Three: the version.** "Incoterms 2020". Without the year it is unclear which edition
applies.

Written out, that gives you something like: "FCA Gardermoen Cargo Terminal, Oslo, Incoterms
2020".

*(The most common omission – say it clearly.)*

The imprecise place is the most common omission. "DAP Norway" says nothing about where the
seller's risk actually ends.

## The ending

*(Slow down. This is why it is worth half an hour.)*

The delivery term is three letters in a contract.

It decides who pays when the container is left standing. When the ship is delayed. When the
goods arrive damaged. And who has to deal with the customs authorities in a country they do
not know.

It also affects the price. A price "DDP Oslo" and a price "FCA Shanghai" are not comparable
until you have added in everything that sits between them.

That is why three letters are worth half an hour.

*(Point to the article for the full table and the remaining 2020 changes.)*

The full table with all eleven rules is on sporretimen.no, along with the overview of what
changed in 2020. It is a useful thing to have open next time you are writing a contract.

*(End card.)*
