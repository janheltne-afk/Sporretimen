**Dale Carnegie** taught public speaking in New York when, in 1936, he published
the book that became one of the best-selling of all time. It is still in print
and still read – not because it is refined, but because what it says about
people largely holds.

## Criticism does not work

The book's first and perhaps most important claim: criticism makes people defend
themselves, not change. The person criticised justifies what they did – and
likes you slightly less. Carnegie opens with stories of criminals who insisted
to the end that they had acted rightly, and the point is that if they will not
take criticism, neither will your colleague.

The alternative is not to let everything pass. It is to start with what is
genuinely good, to talk about your own mistakes before others', and to let the
other person save face.

## Appreciation, not flattery

Carnegie is clear on the difference. Flattery is empty and gets noticed.
Appreciation is sincere and specific: something the other person actually did,
that you actually value. His claim is that the need to feel important is one of
the strongest human drives – and that almost nobody gets enough of it.

## Become interested in others

The most quoted line in the book: you will make more friends in two months by
becoming interested in other people than in two years of trying to get other
people interested in you.

In practice that means remembering names, listening more than you speak, asking
about what the other person cares about, and letting them talk about themselves.
It sounds simple. Carnegie's point is that very few people do it.

## The argument you cannot win

He is categorical: the only way to get the best of an argument is to avoid it.
If you win, the other person has lost face and does not like you. If you lose,
you lose. The alternative is to show respect for their view, admit your own
mistakes quickly, and ask questions that let the other person reach the answer
themselves – so the idea feels like theirs.

## The criticism

The most common objection is that the book teaches people to manipulate.
Carnegie anticipates it himself and insists none of this works unless it is
genuine – a smile you do not mean gets noticed. But the advice *can* be used
cynically, and the book offers few tools for telling the difference.

The examples are from 1930s America, and some are both quaint and
uncomfortable. And the book is not research: it is observation and anecdote.
Much of what it says has since found support in social psychology, but it is
worth knowing it came first, not after.

## What holds up

That criticism puts people on the defensive, that people remember how you made
them feel, and that genuine interest in others is rarer than it should be – none
of that is dated. Read it as a reminder of that, not as a technique.
