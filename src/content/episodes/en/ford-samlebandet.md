The assembly line is the most copied idea in industrial history, and it did not
come from the car industry.

It came from the slaughterhouses.

## The line that ran the other way

In the slaughterhouses of Chicago and Cincinnati in the late nineteenth century,
carcasses hung from overhead rails. A trolley carried them from worker to worker,
and each person made one particular cut before the carcass moved on.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">It was not an assembly line. It was a disassembly line.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>What moved:</b> the workpiece, not the worker.<br /><b>What stayed put:</b> the human being, with one cut and one tool.<br /><b>What Ford saw:</b> that the direction did not matter. If you could take something apart this way, you could put something together in exactly the same way.</p>
</figure>

That is the whole invention, and it looks strikingly simple written down: **move
the workpiece, not the worker.**

## How cars were built before

To see how large this was, you need to know how a car actually came into being
before 1913. And it was not mass production in any sense.

In the 1890s, car building was a craft. Parts were formed in one workshop,
hardened, and distorted in the hardening. Then they were machined again. And
finally, parts coming from different workshops were **filed and worked by hand**
by skilled fitters until they matched one another.

</figure>

## What Ford really invented

Here is the nuance that usually drops out: Ford did not invent interchangeable
parts. He did not invent gauging, and he did not invent machines able to cut
hardened metal.

What he achieved first, in 1908, was **parts precise enough that they did not have
to be finished by hand**. That is the real threshold. Without it an assembly line
is worthless, because the line stops the moment somebody has to stand there
filing.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">The order that made it possible</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>First: precise parts</b><small>Parts must fit without adjustment. This came in 1908, five years before the line.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Then: fixed division of labour</b><small>Each worker performs one operation, in one place, every time.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Then: move the workpiece</b><small>The car is carried past the workers instead of the workers walking to the car.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The order is not incidental, and it is the most important lesson for anyone wanting to copy the model. The assembly line is the last step, not the first. Introduce the line before the parts fit and you have automated nothing – you have merely made it impossible for people to take the time to fix what does not fit.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Highland Park

Ford's first moving line was installed at Highland Park in early 1913, and it was
not for the whole car – it was for flywheel magnetos. From there the principle
spread through the factory.

The consequence for the customer is the part people remember, and it is dramatic.

    <div class="fig__mark" style="left:44%"><b>1914</b><small>$490</small></div>
    <div class="fig__mark" style="left:86%"><b>1924</b><small>$260</small></div>
  </div>
  <p class="fig__ends"><span>Expensive</span><span>Cheap</span></p>
  <figcaption>Between 1908 and 1927 more than 15 million Model Ts were built. The point is not that the car got cheaper – it is that it changed market. It went from something the wealthy bought to something the people building it could buy. That is the social change the assembly line actually set off.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What it cost

Here comes the part that rarely appears in the edifying version of the story, and
it is necessary to understand the rest.

The work at the line was so monotonous and so hard that people left in large
numbers. By late 1913, turnover at Ford was **380 per cent**. The factory
effectively had to hire almost four people to keep one position filled through the
year.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">The five dollar day was not generosity. It was the price of getting people to stay.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>What applied before:</b> $2.34 for a nine-hour day.<br /><b>What came in January 1914:</b> five dollars for an eight-hour day.<br /><b>What it bought:</b> that people were willing to submit to the pace and discipline of the line.<br /><b>Why it is worth saying plainly:</b> the pace did not get gentler. The compensation got higher. Those are two entirely different things, and the difference is the whole point.</p>
</figure>

## What the line does to work

Finally, the part worth carrying forward, and it applies far beyond car
factories.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Who sets the pace</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Without a line</p>
      <h4>The worker</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>You decide when to take the next part</li>
        <li>You can spend extra time on something difficult</li>
        <li>You can catch up later</li>
        <li>Variation in pace is invisible from outside</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">With a line</p>
      <h4>The line</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The next unit arrives regardless</li>
        <li>If you fall behind, everyone sees it</li>
        <li>You cannot catch up – only stop everything</li>
        <li>The pace is a setting, not a negotiation</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>This is what makes the assembly line something other than an efficiency measure. It moves control over the pace of work from the person doing it to the person setting the line. The whole history of working life after 1913 can be read as an argument about that transfer – and the argument continues in the next episode, on Taylor and Toyota.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What is worth taking with you

That the assembly line came from the slaughterhouses, and that it originally ran
the other way.

That the invention is one sentence: move the workpiece, not the worker.

That it was not possible until parts were precise enough that nobody had to file
and hammer them into place.

That time per chassis fell from around 12.5 hours to 93 minutes, and the price
from $850 to $260.

And that turnover was 380 per cent before the five dollar day – because the line
did not only make the work faster, it took the control over pace away from the
worker.
