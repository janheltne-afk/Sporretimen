The times tables are presented as a wall: ten by ten, a hundred facts, memorise
them. It is one reason they sit badly with a good many adults too.

But a hundred is the wrong number. Once you subtract what follows from rules
rather than from memory, a handful is left. This episode goes through where
they go, one table at a time.

## Order is free

The first thing that halves the work is something most people know but few use
deliberately: **3 × 8 and 8 × 3 are the same fact.** Learn one and you have the
other.

It sounds obvious, but it is worth saying out loud while practising. If 7 × 4
gives you trouble, you can fetch it from the four times table instead – and the
fours are one of the easy ones.

## A technique for each table

Every table has its own way in. Some are plain rules, some are shortcuts, and
some are both.

## The doubling chain: 2, 4 and 8

Three of the tables are really one technique, applied once, twice or three
times. If you can double, you have all three.

## The fives, and the sixes that follow from them

**The five times table** is half of the ten times table. 5 × 8 is 80 divided by
two, so 40. That always works, and it is faster than counting up in fives.

The answer also always ends in 0 or 5: in 0 when you multiply by an even
number, in 5 when you multiply by an odd one. That is a free check on the
answer.

**The six times table** hangs straight off it: multiply by five, then add the
number once more. 6 × 8 = 40 + 8 = 48.

The sixes also have a pattern of their own when you multiply by an even number,
and it is one of the prettiest in the whole table:

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">6 multiplied by an even number ends in that same even number – and the tens are half of it.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>See for yourself:</b> 6 × 2 = <b>12</b>, 6 × 4 = <b>24</b>, 6 × 6 = <b>36</b>, 6 × 8 = <b>48</b>. The units digit is the number you multiplied by. The tens digit is half of it.</p>
</figure>

## The nines have the most checks

The nine times table has a reputation for being hard, but it is the best
equipped of them all. The simplest route is to multiply by ten and subtract the
number once: 9 × 7 = 70 − 7 = 63.

On top of that come three checks that catch an error immediately:

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Three ways to check the nines</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">The check</th><th scope="col">How it works</th><th scope="col">9 × 7 = 63</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">The digits</th><td>The digits of the answer always add up to 9</td><td>6 + 3 = 9</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Tens up</th><td>The tens digit is one less than the number you multiplied by</td><td>7 − 1 = 6</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Units down</th><td>The units digit is what is missing up to 10</td><td>10 − 7 = 3</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The three hang together, and not by accident: the tens digit rises by one at each step while the units digit falls by one, so the sum stays at 9. That is why 9, 18, 27, 36 … is a staircase running both ways at once.</figcaption>
</figure>

The finger trick is the same rule done with your hands: hold up ten fingers and
fold down the one matching the number you are multiplying by. For 9 × 7 you
fold the seventh. That leaves six fingers on the left and three on the right –
**63**.

Who came up with the finger trick, we do not know, and we do not claim to. It
is one of those things that has travelled from classroom to classroom without
anyone putting their name on it.

## The square numbers as anchors

The square numbers – 3 × 3, 4 × 4, 6 × 6, 7 × 7, 8 × 8, 9 × 9 – are worth
learning as a short list of their own. Not because they come up so often in
themselves, but because they work as anchors you can step out from.

And then there is one more trick, which takes several of the very hardest facts
at once:

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">If a product sits one step either side of a square, the answer is that square minus one.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Examples:</b> 6 × 8 sits around 7. The answer is 7 × 7 − 1 = 49 − 1 = <b>48</b>. And 7 × 9 sits around 8: 8 × 8 − 1 = 64 − 1 = <b>63</b>. It works everywhere: 4 × 6 = 5 × 5 − 1 = 24.</p>
</figure>

This is not a curiosity. 6 × 8 and 7 × 9 are among the facts people get wrong
most often, and here both fall out of the same rule.

## What is actually left

Work through the techniques above and strikingly little remains to memorise.
Most of the facts are now covered by at least one rule, and several of them by
two or three.

What tends to sit worst at the end is a small group in the middle of the table –
typically **6 × 7, 6 × 8, 7 × 8** and **7 × 9**. They are a long way from the
easy rules and have no conspicuous patterns. Two of them are admittedly taken
by the square trick above.

These few are the ones worth practising deliberately. And the point there is to
retrieve them from memory, not to read them again: pose yourself the fact, wait
until the answer arrives, and check. That is harder work than reading a table,
and that is precisely why it sticks better. Spread the sessions across several
days rather than doing everything in one – spaced repetition is one of the most
widely used techniques in study skills, and the times tables are close to an
ideal use for it.

## Why the techniques work

This is the part that keeps the rules from becoming new things to remember.

None of them is a trick in the real sense. They fall out of two things: that
multiplication can be split up (7 × 8 is the same as 5 × 8 plus 2 × 8), and that
the decimal system makes ten an easy number to multiply by. That is why the
fives are half the tens, and why the nines are the tens minus one round.

The square trick has a simple explanation too: 7 × 9 is 8 − 1 multiplied by
8 + 1, and when you expand that, everything vanishes except 8 × 8 minus 1. The
same holds for any other pair sitting equally far from the same square.

Grasp that and you have not learned ten rules. You have learned two, and ten
ways of using them.
