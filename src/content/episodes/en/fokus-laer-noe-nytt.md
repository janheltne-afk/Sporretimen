Most people recognise it: you sit down to do one thing, and discover twenty minutes
later that you are doing something else entirely. The question is what causes it –
and whether it is really your fault.

In this Learn something new episode Jan Sindre goes through two books that answer
that quite differently.

**Stolen Focus** (2022) by the journalist **Johann Hari** looks outwards: at
technology, working life, sleep, food and the environment around us. His central
point is that our attention is not merely slipping – it is being taken from us by
forces that profit from it, and that the problem therefore cannot be solved by
self-discipline alone.

**Hyperfocus** (2018) by **Chris Bailey** looks inwards: at how attention actually
works, how much of the day runs on autopilot, and how you deliberately drop into
*hyperfocus* on a single task. Bailey is just as interested in the opposite –
*scatterfocus*, letting the mind wander – and in the fact that the brain needs both.

We look at what the two agree on, where they pull in different directions, and what
is actually usable in an ordinary working day.

## The two modes Bailey describes

What surprises most people in *Hyperfocus* is that half the book is about the
opposite of concentrating.

<figure class="fig fig--cycle">
  <p class="fig__title">The brain needs both</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__step">Mode 1</span><b>Hyperfocus</b><small>All attention on one task. This is where work gets done, and where you notice time moving fast.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">Mode 2</span><b>Scatterfocus</b><small>Letting the mind wander on purpose. This is where things connect, and where the rest is.</small></li>
  </ol>
  <p class="fig__loop">↻ And then back to the first</p>
  <figcaption>Bailey's point is that the second is not wasted time but something the brain depends on to tie together what it has taken in. Good ideas rarely arrive in the middle of hyperfocus – they arrive in the shower afterwards.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">It is easier to remove a distraction in advance than to resist it in the moment.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>What lies underneath:</b> both books end up here, though they come at it from opposite sides. You lose when you have to decide against something over and over through the day. You win when the decision was made once, before you sat down.</p>
</figure>
