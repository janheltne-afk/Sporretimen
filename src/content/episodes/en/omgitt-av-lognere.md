The last book in Erikson's series is about lying. It is a subject with a great
deal of research behind it, and one where almost everything people think they
know is wrong – so we spend the episode on what is actually documented.

## How good are we?

The most robust finding in the field comes from a meta-analysis by Charles Bond
and Bella DePaulo in 2006, which pooled more than 200 studies. The conclusion:
people correctly judge whether someone is lying about **54 per cent** of the
time.

A coin flip gives 50. So we are marginally better than chance, and the small
edge comes mostly from being better at recognising truth than at recognising
lies.

Worse: this holds for people who believe they are good at it too. Police
officers, customs officers, judges and psychologists are right about as often as
everyone else. What they have more of is **confidence** – and in some studies
accuracy is lower among those who are most certain.

## Why body language does not work

The widespread idea is that lying leaks out: averted eyes, restless hands,
sweating, hesitation. The problem is that these are signs of **discomfort**, not
of lying.

And they get confused for a simple reason: being suspected is uncomfortable. A
person confronted with something serious becomes nervous whether they are guilty
or innocent. The nervousness comes from the situation, not from the truth value.

Some of the best-known signals do not hold at all. The notion that liars look
away is close to refuted – capable liars often hold more eye contact, precisely
because they know what is expected.

## The truth default

We believe people by default. It is called the truth default, and it is the
reason we are deceived more often than we think.

It is worth saying that this is mostly a **sensible** strategy. A society that
presumes lying does not function, and the vast majority of statements we
encounter are in fact true. The cost of distrusting everyone is higher than the
cost of being deceived occasionally.

## What works somewhat better

The research, particularly the work of **Aldert Vrij**, points in one direction:
stop watching the person, and look at the **content**.

- **Ask for many details.** True accounts have more and richer detail, because
  they are retrieved from memory rather than constructed.
- **Ask for the account in reverse.** That is cognitively demanding, and
  demanding tasks are harder to complete while also maintaining a construction.
- **Ask unexpected questions.** A prepared story covers the expected.
- **Check against something external.** Times, receipts, other witnesses.
- **Hold back what you know.** Produce it late, and see whether the story
  collides with it.

The common thread is that you are looking for **contradictions and
verifiability**, not for nervous tells.

## And the polygraph

The polygraph measures pulse, perspiration and breathing – that is, arousal. It
cannot distinguish nervousness from lying, and the error rate is high enough
that results are not used as evidence in Norwegian courts. That it was invented
by the same man behind the DISC model is a curiosity that makes neither of them
more reliable.

*The content is a general walk through research on the subject, not a method for
assessing any specific person.*
