How is it possible to remember 52 cards in the right order? The answer is neither
talent nor a photographic memory – it is a **system**, and it can be learned.

In this Learn something new episode Jan Sindre goes through the memory techniques
behind it: how you build a **memory palace**, how each card is turned into a distinct
image, and how those images are placed along a route you already know. We also look
at the **PAO system**, where every card gets a person, an action and an object – so
that several cards can be packed into a single memory.

The point is not the deck of cards in itself. It is understanding why the brain
remembers images and places so much better than numbers and symbols – and how you can
use the same trick on names, course material, shopping lists and presentations.

## The memory palace, step by step

The method is old – it is known from antiquity – and it swaps something the brain is
bad at for something it is good at. Numbers and symbols slide apart. Places and
images do not.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Four links</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Pick a route you already know</b><small>The house you grew up in, the way to work, the rooms in the flat. What matters is that the order sits without your having to think.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Turn each card into an image</b><small>Not “queen of spades”, but a particular person or thing you can see. The sharper and the stranger, the better it sticks.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Put the image somewhere on the route</b><small>The first card in the hallway, the next on the kitchen counter. Let the image do something to the place – it is the interaction that is remembered.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Walk the route in your head</b><small>Retrieving it is just walking the same way again. The cards come in the right order because the rooms do.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Note that none of the links is about repeating anything many times. The order is not something you remember – it is already in the route, and you have known that for years.</figcaption>
</figure>

## PAO – packing three cards into one image

To manage a whole deck, 52 separate images is a lot of places to fill. PAO solves that
by giving each card three roles, and then combining three cards into a single scene.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Person, action, object</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Role</th><th scope="col">What it is</th><th scope="col">Taken from</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Person</th><td>Who is doing something in the image</td><td>The first card you draw</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Action</th><td>What is being done</td><td>The card you draw next</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Object</th><td>What it is being done with</td><td>The third card</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Every card has all three roles ready in advance, and which one is used depends on where in the trio the card lands. Three cards then become one stop on the route instead of three – and a deck needs seventeen stops, not fifty-two. The list is one you have to make yourself: it works because the images are yours, not because they are the right ones.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">The point is not the deck of cards. It is that the same trick works on everything else.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>The same method, other uses:</b> names you want to remember in a meeting, the points of a presentation you would rather not read off the page, course material for an exam, the shopping list. The deck is simply the hardest exercise – and therefore the one that shows most clearly that the system works.</p>
</figure>
