**Cal Newport** is a professor of computer science at Georgetown, and *Deep
Work* (2016) is the book that turned “deep work” into a term. His claim is
simple and uncomfortable: the ability to concentrate for long stretches on
something hard is getting rarer and rarer – and precisely for that reason, more
and more valuable.

## Deep and shallow

**Deep work** is effort at full concentration, without distraction, on
something that takes everything you have – and that creates value hard to
replicate. **Shallow work** is the rest: email, meetings, updates, logistics.
Necessary, but easy to do, and easy to replace.

Newport's point is that most knowledge workers spend the bulk of the day on the
shallow and believe that is the job. It is not.

## Why it is worth more

Because it is getting rarer. Open-plan offices, always-on chat and a phone in
the pocket systematically train us to switch attention every minute. Anyone who
can still sit for four hours with one thing has an advantage that grows each
year.

## Four ways to do it

Newport insists there is no single correct recipe:

- **Monastic** – cut all shallow work, permanently. Few can.
- **Bimodal** – long stretches (days, weeks) deep, the rest open.
- **Rhythmic** – a fixed block every day, same time. The one most people can
  manage.
- **Journalistic** – deep work in whatever gap appears. Takes practice.

Common to all are **rituals**: same place, same start, same rules, so that
getting going does not take willpower.

## Tolerating boredom

The most practical part. Concentration is a skill, and it weakens every time you
fill a boring moment with a screen. Newport's advice is to schedule when you
will be online, not when you will be offline – and to let the boredom stand the
rest of the time.

## The criticism

The book is written for people who control their own time. A nurse, a teacher
or someone in customer service cannot choose the monastic rhythm, and Newport
has little to say to them. The advice to leave social media rests on his own
experience, and ignores the fact that many jobs genuinely require it.

The arguments are also largely anecdotal – Carl Jung, Bill Gates, a professor
who got tenure. The book predates most of the research on attention and
interruption, and leans on it lightly.

What holds up is the distinction. Knowing the difference between deep and
shallow, and noticing how much of the day goes to the latter, is worth the book
on its own.
