**Daniel Kahneman** received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2002, as a
psychologist. The work he and **Amos Tversky** did from the late 1960s showed
that people do not make decisions the way economic theory assumed – and that
the deviations are not random but systematic. *Thinking, Fast and Slow* is his
summary of a working life.

## The two systems

The image the book is built around: the brain works in two ways.

**System 1** is fast, automatic and takes no effort. It recognises faces, reads
the words on a sign before you have decided to read them, and answers 4 to
2 + 2. It is always on.

**System 2** is slow, deliberate and takes concentration. It is what you use to
multiply 17 by 24, to fill in a form, or to keep your composure in a difficult
conversation. It is effortful, and so we economise on it.

The problem is not that System 1 exists. The problem is that it answers
questions it cannot really answer, and that the answer arrives so quickly and
so confidently that System 2 is rarely asked to check.

## Some of the biases

- **Anchoring.** A number you have just seen affects what you guess afterwards
  – even when you know the number was random.
- **Availability.** We judge how common something is by how easily we can call
  up an example. So we overestimate risks that get media coverage, and
  underestimate those that do not.
- **Loss aversion.** Losing a hundred stings roughly twice as much as winning a
  hundred pleases. It explains a lot of behaviour that otherwise looks
  irrational.
- **Framing.** "Nine in ten survive" and "one in ten die" are the same
  information, but they lead people to choose differently.
- **WYSIATI** – *what you see is all there is*. We build a coherent story from
  what we happen to know, and do not notice what is missing.
- **The planning fallacy.** We estimate time and cost as if everything will go
  to plan, even when we know it never has before.

## Prospect theory

The work that earned the Nobel Prize. In short: people do not evaluate
outcomes by how much they end up with, but by the change from where they stand
now. And the curve is steeper going down than going up. That is why the same
money means different things depending on whether it is experienced as a gain
or a loss.

## Two selves

One of the most thought-provoking parts is about memory. The **experiencing
self** is you while something is happening. The **remembering self** is you
telling the story afterwards. They do not agree.

The remembering self hardly cares how long something lasted, but cares a great
deal about the most intense moment and about the ending. That means we make
choices for the future based on memories that systematically distort what we
actually experienced.

## What has not held up

This belongs here. The book came out in 2011, just before the replication
crisis in psychology. The chapter on **priming** – that small, imperceptible
cues steer behaviour to a significant degree – rests on studies that have since
failed to replicate.

Kahneman himself wrote in 2017 that he had placed too much weight on those
findings, and that readers should not rely on that chapter. That is unusually
honest of an author, and worth repeating when recommending the book.

The core findings on anchoring, availability, loss aversion and framing stand
far stronger, and have been replicated many times.

## Worth knowing

The book is long and demands concentration – fittingly. It offers no checklist
for avoiding the errors, and Kahneman is clear that he does not believe you can
think your way out of them. What he does think is possible is recognising the
situations where the errors are likely, and building in a check there.
