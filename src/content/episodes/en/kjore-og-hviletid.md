Most occupations have working hours agreed between employer and employee. A lorry
driver's working hours are something else: laid down in regulation, measured by a
device in the dashboard, and checkable at the roadside several weeks back in time.

The reason is simple. A tired accountant makes a mistake in a spreadsheet. A tired
driver in forty tonnes makes one at a hundred kilometres an hour.

This episode goes through how the system works, and what the rules actually say.

## Who it applies to

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">When the rules bite</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Type of transport</th><th scope="col">Threshold</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Goods</th><td>The vehicle's maximum authorised mass, including trailer or semi-trailer, exceeds 3.5 tonnes.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Passengers</th><td>The vehicle is built, or permanently adapted, for transport of more than nine people including the driver.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Light vans across borders</th><td>From 1 July 2026, cross-border goods and cabotage transport in vehicles above 2.5 tonnes is covered as well.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The last row is the newest, and it surprises many small operators: a three-tonne van running internationally is now inside the rules. There are in addition a number of exemptions – among them vehicles with a top speed below 40 km/h, emergency vehicles, and certain vehicles under 7.5 tonnes in non-commercial transport.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The tachograph

The device in the dashboard is what the whole system hangs on. Vehicles registered
after 9 December 2006 must have a digital tachograph.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">The tachograph distinguishes between four things, and it is the driver who has to tell it which one applies.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Driving</b> is recorded automatically when the vehicle moves.<br /><b>Other work</b> is unloading, loading, paperwork, maintenance – work that is not driving.<br /><b>Period of availability</b> is waiting where you are not working, but cannot use the time freely either.<br /><b>Break or rest</b> is the only thing that counts as a break.<br /><b>The point:</b> only the first records itself. The other three must be selected manually, and that is where most unintentional breaches arise – not from driving too long, but from forgetting to switch mode.</p>
</figure>

The driver must also register the country where the driving starts and ends. In
multi-manning – at least two drivers in the vehicle – the active driver's card must
always be in card slot number 1.

## The driver card

The card is the driver's identity in the system. It is personal, and it is what
ties the recordings to a human being rather than to a vehicle.

To be issued with a driver card you need a valid driving licence and a Norwegian
national identity number. If you drive a vehicle with a digital tachograph within
the rules, you must have a card.

## The driving period

Here come the numbers, and it is worth taking them in the right order.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">From hour to week</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Nine hours a day</b><small>Daily driving time must not exceed nine hours.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Ten hours, twice a week</b><small>Up to twice a week it may be extended to ten hours.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>56 hours in a week</b><small>Total driving time in one week.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>90 hours over two weeks</b><small>Across two consecutive weeks the ceiling is 90 hours in total.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Note how the four interlock. Drive 56 hours one week and only 34 remain for the next – however well rested you are. There are ceilings at four levels at once, and you breach the rule the moment any one of them goes.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The break

This is the rule most people have heard of, and the one most often quoted wrongly.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">How the break may be taken</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">In one</p>
      <h4>45 minutes</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>After no more than four and a half hours of driving</li>
        <li>Must be spent on rest</li>
        <li>Cannot be used for other work</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Split</p>
      <h4>15 + 30 minutes</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>First part at least 15 minutes</li>
        <li>Second part at least 30 minutes</li>
        <li>The order <em>cannot</em> be swapped</li>
        <li>The final part must always be at least 30 minutes</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>That last point catches the most people. Take 30 minutes first and 15 at the end, and you have not met the requirement – even though the total is the same. And a break is a break: 45 minutes of unloading counts as other work, not as rest.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Daily rest

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Three lawful variants</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Variant</th><th scope="col">Condition</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Normal, unbroken</th><td>At least 11 consecutive hours.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Split</th><td>At least 3 hours, then at least 9 hours.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Reduced</th><td>At least 9 hours, but no more than three times between two weekly rests.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The split variant adds up to 12 hours, more than the unbroken 11. That is not an error in the rules – it is the price of splitting. And the reduced version is a quota, not an entitlement: use up the three, and the rest of the week has to be taken on eleven.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Weekly rest

After no more than six working days a weekly rest must be taken. The main rule is
45 hours.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Reduced weekly rest, and what it costs</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>45 hours is the norm</b><small>The normal weekly rest period.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>24 hours every second week</b><small>The weekly rest may be reduced to 24 hours every second week.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The difference must be compensated</b><small>The hours you cut have to be made up.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Within three weeks, in one block</b><small>Compensation must happen within three weeks, and be taken together – not piecemeal.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The requirement that compensation be taken in one block matters more than it looks. Without it, the missing hours could have been spread out as a little extra rest here and there, which does not give the same recovery as one continuous period.</figcaption>
</figure>

For international goods transport there is an exception to the two-week rule: over
a four-week period the driver may have two normal and two reduced weekly rests.

## What the Mobility Package changed

Here comes the part that is least about hours and most about what the job actually
feels like.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Two changes that moved the responsibility</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Where you sleep</p>
      <h4>Not in the vehicle</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>A normal weekly rest, or rest over 45 hours, may not be taken in the vehicle</li>
        <li>It must be taken in a gender-neutral place</li>
        <li>With satisfactory facilities for sleep and sanitary needs</li>
        <li>All accommodation costs are covered by the employer</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Getting home</p>
      <h4>At least every fourth week</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The undertaking must organise the work so the driver can get home</li>
        <li>At least every fourth week</li>
        <li>To take a normal weekly rest</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Note where the duties sit. Both are framed as requirements on the <em>undertaking</em>, not on the driver. That is deliberate: a driver far from home, in a fiercely competitive market, has little practical ability to demand this personally. So it is the employer who must organise and pay.</figcaption>
</figure>

One small, practical change in the same round: a reduced weekly rest may now be
interrupted on the same conditions as a normal daily rest when the vehicle is
carried by train or ferry.

## Why the system looks the way it does

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">The rules are not written to protect the driver from themselves. They are written to protect the driver from the pressure.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>The problem they solve:</b> in a market where margins are thin and the job is urgent, it will always pay someone to drive a little longer.<br /><b>Why an agreement will not do:</b> whoever refuses loses the job to whoever says yes. Without a shared limit, everyone is pushed downwards.<br /><b>Hence the measuring device:</b> a rule without recording is a recommendation. The tachograph makes it verifiable – weeks later, and in another country.</p>
</figure>

## What is worth taking with you

That the threshold is 3.5 tonnes for goods and nine people for passenger transport
– and that 2.5 tonnes now applies to cross-border transport.

That nine hours is the driving time, ten twice a week, 56 in a week and 90 over
two.

That the break is 45 minutes after four and a half hours, and that if it is split,
the long part must come last.

That daily rest is eleven hours, or three plus nine, and that nine hours is a quota
of three between weekly rests.

That weekly rest is 45 hours, can be reduced to 24 every second week, and that the
difference must be made up in one block within three weeks.

And that a normal weekly rest is not taken in the cab, that the employer pays, and
that the driver must be able to get home at least every fourth week.

*This is a general account of the rules, not legal advice on a specific case. For
binding detail, the Norwegian regulations on driving and rest time and tachographs
apply, with the underlying EEA regulations. Rules and exemptions change, so always
check current information with the Norwegian Public Roads Administration.*
