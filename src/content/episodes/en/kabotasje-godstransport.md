You have seen them: a Lithuanian lorry on the E6, carrying Norwegian goods,
between two Norwegian towns. Many people assume that is illegal. As a rule it is
not – but it is tightly regulated, and the rules are more precise than the debate
tends to be.

This episode goes through what cabotage actually is, exactly where the line runs,
and why the arrangement exists.

## What cabotage is

The definition is shorter than you would think.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Cabotage is transport between two places in a country other than the one the operator belongs to.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Is cabotage:</b> a Polish operator carries goods from Oslo to Bergen.<br /><b>Is not cabotage:</b> the same operator carries goods from Warsaw to Oslo. That is international transport, and it is free.<br /><b>Also not cabotage:</b> a Norwegian operator drives Oslo–Bergen. That is simply domestic transport.</p>
</figure>

The distinction is therefore not between Norwegian and foreign drivers. It is
between *international* transport, which is free within the EEA, and *domestic*
transport in a country you do not belong to, which is limited.

## Three operations in seven days

The main rule is easy to remember, and it has three parts that must all be met.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">The conditions for lawful cabotage</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>There must have been an international journey first</b><small>The cabotage follows from a journey into Norway with international goods. You cannot drive in empty and start taking domestic work.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Up to three domestic operations</b><small>No more, however short they are.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Within seven days</b><small>The clock starts when the international goods are unloaded in Norway.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>With the same vehicle</b><small>The quota attaches to the vehicle, not to the operator.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>One of the three operations may be carried out as transit through another country, without taking on new international goods. Otherwise this is a hard limit: if the inbound journey cannot be documented, every domestic operation is unlawful – including the first one.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The cooling-off period

This is the most important change from the EU Mobility Package, which entered into
force on 1 November 2022. Before it, an operator could in practice make a short
run across the border and start seven new days.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Before and after the Mobility Package</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Before</p>
      <h4>Seven days, over again</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Three operations in seven days</li>
        <li>Out of the country</li>
        <li>Back in – and three new operations</li>
        <li>In practice, continuous operation</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">From 1 November 2022</p>
      <h4>Four-day cooling-off</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Three operations in seven days, as before</li>
        <li>Then four days when the vehicle cannot do cabotage in Norway</li>
        <li>The cooling-off attaches to the vehicle</li>
        <li>Only then can new operations begin</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The purpose of the cooling-off period is to separate temporary cabotage from permanent presence. Without it, a vehicle could in reality operate continuously in the Norwegian market under rules meant for occasional use. Note that the cooling-off follows the vehicle – not the driver and not the company.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The documentation

The rule is worth little without something to check it against, and this is where
most cases are actually decided.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">What must be produced</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Document</th><th scope="col">What it shows</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Community licence</th><td>That the operator is entitled to carry out international transport in the EEA.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Driver attestation</th><td>For drivers from outside the EEA – that they are lawfully employed by the operator.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Consignment note for the inbound journey</th><td>That there really was an international transport, and when the goods were unloaded.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Consignment note for each operation</th><td>How many domestic operations have been carried out, and when.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The third item is the key. Without a documented international journey in, there is no basis for cabotage at all, and the whole chain falls. That is why roadside enforcement is in practice more often about paperwork than about the driving itself.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Buses are a different matter

Many people fall into a trap here, because passenger transport has an entirely
different rulebook.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Goods against people</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Goods</p>
      <h4>Counted in operations</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Three operations</li>
        <li>Seven days</li>
        <li>Four-day cooling-off</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Passengers, occasional services</p>
      <h4>Counted in days</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>No more than 20 continuous days</li>
        <li>No more than 30 days in a calendar year</li>
        <li>Regular services only as part of an international route</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The difference in unit of measure is worth noting: goods transport is limited by <em>number of operations</em>, passenger transport by <em>time</em>. That follows from the fact that a coach assignment can last a fortnight, while a lorry job typically lasts a day.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The fines

From 1 March 2024 administrative fines apply to a range of breaches. A missing
driver attestation or licence documentation can bring a fine of up to NOK 60,000,
and eight different cabotage breaches carry fines – among them exceeding the
seven-day window, carrying out more than three operations, and incomplete
documentation for the operations.

It is worth noting what this does to the incentives: an on-the-spot fine also
reaches operators who are hard to pursue across borders after the fact.

## Why the rules exist

This is the part missing from most discussions, and without it the rules are just
arbitrary numbers.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Two concerns pulling opposite ways</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">For opening up</p>
      <h4>Empty return runs are waste</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>A lorry that has delivered in Oslo is going back anyway</li>
        <li>If it runs empty, the trip is pure cost and pure emissions</li>
        <li>Free movement of services is a founding principle of the EEA</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">For limiting it</p>
      <h4>Wage levels are not equal</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Cost levels vary sharply between EEA countries</li>
        <li>Without a limit the domestic market can be taken over permanently</li>
        <li>Norwegian road freight has a generally applicable collective agreement</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The rules are the compromise between these two. The numbers – three, seven, four – are not derived from any principle. They were negotiated, which is why they have changed and may change again. Understanding that makes the next round of amendments easier to read.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What is hard

Finally, the honest caveat: the rules are easier to write than to enforce.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Three practical problems</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Enforcement is by spot check</b><small>A vehicle has to be stopped for the paperwork to be examined, and the road network is long.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>History is hard to see</b><small>At the roadside you see today's journey. Establishing how many operations have been run over the past seven days requires documentation that is supposed to be there – and that is the weakest link.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Follow-up crosses borders</b><small>The operator is registered in another country, with another inspectorate and another legal system.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>This explains the direction the rules have taken: more documentation duties, on-the-spot fines, and in time digital tachographs that make border crossings traceable. The aim is not stricter limits, but limits that can actually be checked.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What is worth taking with you

That cabotage does not mean "foreign lorry", but domestic transport carried out by
an operator based somewhere else.

That the limit is three operations in seven days, with the same vehicle, after a
documented international journey in.

That the four-day cooling-off period is what separates temporary cabotage from
permanent presence.

And that buses count days, not operations.

*This is a general account of the rules, not legal advice on a specific case. For
binding detail, the Norwegian professional transport legislation and the
underlying EEA regulations apply. Rules and fine levels change, so always check
current information with the Norwegian Public Roads Administration.*
