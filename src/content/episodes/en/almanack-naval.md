**Naval Ravikant** founded AngelList and invested early in a long list of
companies. The book is not written by him: **Eric Jorgenson** collected and
organised what Naval has said in tweets, podcasts and interviews over a decade.
Jorgenson gives it away – it is freely available online.

## Wealth, money and status

Three things that get conflated. **Wealth** is assets that earn while you sleep:
businesses, code, media, equity. **Money** is how we transfer wealth. **Status**
is your rank in a hierarchy.

The distinction matters because wealth and status are different kinds of game.
Wealth is positive-sum – many people can get rich at once. Status is zero-sum:
someone has to go down for you to go up. Naval's advice is to play the wealth
game and ignore the status game, because the latter makes enemies without making
anyone richer.

## Specific knowledge

The central concept, and the vaguest. **Specific knowledge** is what you cannot
learn through education, because it cannot be put in a curriculum. It is where
your curiosity and a genuine interest have taken you – something you learned
because you could not help it.

The marker is that it feels like play to you and work to others. And because it
is hard to replace, it is also hard to compete down in price.

## Accountability

You rarely get upside without standing under your own name and carrying the risk
that comes with it. Naval's point is that people want to share the upside but
not the downside, and that the willingness to own the downside is precisely what
separates. It applies as much to a self-employed joiner as to a founder.

## Leverage

The most usable section. Effort alone is capped by the hours in the day.
Leverage is what multiplies it, and it comes in four kinds:

- **Labour** – people working for you. Oldest, hardest, and requires someone to
  give you the job.
- **Capital** – money at work. Requires someone to give you the money.
- **Code** – software that does the job while you sleep.
- **Media** – something you make once, read or heard an unlimited number of
  times.

The last two he calls permissionless leverage: nobody has to approve your
writing code or making things. That is the most important change of our era.

## Hourly rate

A concrete move: decide on an hourly rate that is unreasonably high, and refuse
consistently to spend time on anything cheaper than it. If it is faster to pay
someone to do it, pay them. The purpose is not stinginess but forcing yourself
to spend time on what actually produces something.

## Long-term games

“Play long-term games with long-term people.” Compounding works not only on
money but on trust, reputation and relationships – and only if you stay long
enough with the same people.

## Happiness

The book's second half is about something else entirely. Naval treats happiness
as a trainable skill rather than a trait. The core claim: **a desire is a
contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want.**
That does not mean wanting nothing, but choosing few, large desires
deliberately – and knowing you pay for them in unease along the way.

## Worth noting

The book is a collection of aphorisms, not an argument. That makes it easy to
open anywhere and hard to use: “find your specific knowledge” is true and nearly
impossible to act on.

The larger objection is selection bias. This is advice from one person who
succeeded extremely well in Silicon Valley in a very particular period. We hear
nothing from the many who followed the same principles and got nowhere, and the
book does not discuss luck at all. Read it as a well-phrased way of thinking –
not as a recipe that produces the same outcome.
