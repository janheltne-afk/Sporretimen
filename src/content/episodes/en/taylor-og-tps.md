The previous episode ended on a question: who sets the pace of work?

This is the story of two answers to it. They sit a century and an ocean apart,
they are treated as opposites – and they have more in common than either camp
likes to admit.

## Taylor and the stopwatch

**Frederick Winslow Taylor** published *The Principles of Scientific Management*
in 1911. The idea was that work is not a craft each worker shapes for themselves,
but a problem that can be measured and solved.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">The method, in four steps</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Break the task down</b><small>Divide the work into individual motions that can be observed separately.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Time every motion</b><small>With a stopwatch, repeatedly, across several workers.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Find the one best way</b><small>Assemble the fastest motions into a single prescribed method.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Teach it, and require it</b><small>The worker no longer finds their own way. The method is decided somewhere else.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Note what happens in the last step. The knowledge of how the work is best done is moved out of the head of the person doing it and into a planning department. That is the core of Taylorism, and it is also why it was so hated: it makes experience worthless as a bargaining chip.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The story of Schmidt

Scientific management has one founding story, and anyone who has taken a
management course has heard it.

At Bethlehem Steel, around the turn of the century, workers were loading an
average of **12.5 tonnes of pig iron** per man per day, for wages of $1.15.
Taylor describes how he picked out one worker – he calls him **Schmidt** – and
through careful instruction got him up to **47 tonnes** a day.

The story is still used as proof of what the method can achieve.

## When somebody checked it

This is where it gets interesting, and this part is missing from most accounts.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">When researchers went back to the sources, the founding story turned out to be partly fabricated.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>The man existed:</b> "Schmidt" was in reality Henry Noll.<br /><b>But the account did not hold:</b> it is described as fictionalised and error-riddled.<br /><b>And something had been removed:</b> when Taylor retold the episode in his congressional testimony, the revolt among the pig-iron handlers and the mass dismissals that preceded it had disappeared from the narrative.<br /><b>The scale was smaller too:</b> the reorganisation at Bethlehem is described in hindsight as a modest affair, far less dramatic than either Taylor or his critics later made it.</p>
</figure>

This is worth dwelling on, because it is rare for so central an idea to rest so
heavily on a single anecdote. Scientific management was not built on a dataset. It
was built on a story about one man, told by the man selling the method.

That does not mean everything Taylor said was wrong. That work can be measured,
and that measurement reveals waste, holds up. But it is worth knowing that the
example which convinced a whole generation of managers does not survive source
criticism.

## The resistance

Taylorism met hard resistance, and not only in theory. At **Watertown Arsenal**
its introduction led to a strike, and it ended in congressional hearings. The
method was eventually banned in United States government arsenals.

The reason for the resistance was not that people were against efficiency. It was
the stopwatch itself: being stood over and timed, by a stranger, while you work.

## Toyota, and the opposite premise

Half a century later, in Japan, a system is built that measures work just as
precisely – but inverts one premise.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Who holds the improvement</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Taylor</p>
      <h4>The worker is the object</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The planners decide the method</li>
        <li>The worker carries it out</li>
        <li>Deviation is an error</li>
        <li>The knowledge sits in the staff function</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Toyota</p>
      <h4>The worker is the source</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The standard is the starting point, not the answer</li>
        <li>Whoever does the job proposes the change</li>
        <li>Surfacing a problem is wanted</li>
        <li>The knowledge sits at the machine</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The difference is not how closely it is measured – both measure in seconds. The difference is who holds the watch, and what happens when something does not match. For Taylor a deviation is a disciplinary matter. For Toyota a deviation is information.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The andon cord

The clearest expression of that is a cord above the line.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Any worker can stop the entire production line.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>What it means in practice:</b> see a problem, pull. The line halts, and people come to look at it with you.<br /><b>Why it is radical:</b> at Ford, stopping the line was the most expensive thing a human being could do. Here it is a duty.<br /><b>The logic behind it:</b> a fault allowed to pass gets more expensive at every station it passes. Stopping now is cheaper than repairing later.<br /><b>And what makes it a management device:</b> it is a statement of trust that is also an instrument of measurement. Every time somebody pulls, management knows exactly where the problem sits.</p>
</figure>

## How extreme it actually is

Here it is worth being honest, because TPS is often presented as the humane
alternative to the line. The system is humane in some ways and rock-hard in
others.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">What makes TPS demanding to work in</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Feature</th><th scope="col">What it involves</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Takt time</th><td>Work is divided into cycles of seconds, set by customer demand – not by what is comfortable.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Standardised work</th><td>Every motion is described and the sequence fixed. It is more detailed than Taylor ever got.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Little slack</th><td>Inventory is removed deliberately. With the buffers gone, a problem propagates immediately.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Kaizen</th><td>Improvement is continuous. Today's standard is next week's starting point.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Visibility</th><td>Everything is meant to be visible: status, deviations, delays. Yours included.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Look at the last two together. When improvement is continuous and everything is visible, there is by definition no state in which the work is good enough. It is a system with no resting point built into it.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The criticism

The sharpest objection to TPS is not that it is inhumane. It is more uncomfortable
than that.

<figure class="fig fig--cycle">
  <p class="fig__title">"Management by stress", as the criticism describes it</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__step">1</span><b>Remove the slack</b><small>Inventory, buffers and spare staffing are taken away.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">2</span><b>Problems become visible</b><small>Without a buffer the line stops where it fails. That is the intention.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">3</span><b>The problem gets solved</b><small>The system genuinely improves. This part is real.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">4</span><b>Then it is tightened again</b><small>The gain is taken out as new, lower slack.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">5</span><b>And the pressure is back</b><small>At a higher level of performance, with the same felt strain.</small></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The point of the criticism is not that the system does not work – it does. The point is that the strain is held constant on purpose, because it is the strain that surfaces the problems. The improvement accrues to the business. The pressure stays with the person doing the work.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The uncomfortable kinship

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Both systems measure work down to seconds, and both standardise every motion.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>What actually separates them:</b> who owns the standard, and what happens when somebody finds a better way.<br /><b>For Taylor:</b> the standard comes from above and is to be followed.<br /><b>For Toyota:</b> the standard is a hypothesis, and whoever does the job is expected to improve it.<br /><b>Why the difference is real:</b> a system where you can change your own method is a different thing to live in than one where you cannot.<br /><b>But also why it is fragile:</b> introduce the tools without the authority – the stopwatch without the cord – and you have Taylorism with Japanese vocabulary.</p>
</figure>

That last point is the most practical lesson in the episode. Lean rollouts that
fail usually do not fail on the tools. They fail because the right to improve was
never handed over.

## What is worth taking with you

That Taylorism's founding story about Schmidt and the pig iron does not survive
source criticism, and that Taylor himself edited out the revolt and the dismissals
when he retold it.

That resistance to the stopwatch was strong enough to trigger a strike and
congressional hearings.

That Toyota measures just as closely, but inverts the premise: the worker is the
source of improvement, not its object.

That the andon cord is both a statement of trust and an instrument of measurement.

That TPS is a system with no resting point built in, and that this is deliberate.

And that the difference between the two lies not in the tools, but in who owns the
standard.
