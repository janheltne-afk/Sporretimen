Most of us learned one way to add numbers: stack them, start on the right,
remember to carry.

That method is good on paper. In your head it is close to the worst thing you
can do.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">The same sum, two directions</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Right to left</p>
      <h4>The way you write</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Start with the units, work backwards</li>
        <li>Hold a carry while you go</li>
        <li>The answer arrives back to front – units first</li>
        <li>You know nothing about the size until you finish</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Left to right</p>
      <h4>The way you think</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Start with the big part – hundreds, then tens</li>
        <li>The answer builds in the order you say it</li>
        <li>You have an approximate answer immediately</li>
        <li>Nothing to hold in mind going backwards</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The difference is not mathematical – both give the same answer. The difference is what your head has to hold at once. Going left to right you can say "eighty-something" before you finish, and the rest is an adjustment. Going right to left you sit on a digit you cannot use for anything until the whole sum is done.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Four routes to the same answer

Take **47 + 38**. There are at least four ways, and all of them beat stacking
the numbers.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">47 + 38 = 85</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Technique</th><th scope="col">How it goes</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Left to right</th><td>40 + 30 = 70, then 7 + 8 = 15. 70 + 15 = 85</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Round and correct</th><td>47 + 40 = 87. You added 2 too many: 87 − 2 = 85</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Fill up to the ten</th><td>47 + 3 = 50. That leaves 35: 50 + 35 = 85</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Shift between them</th><td>Take 2 off 47 and give it to 38: 45 + 40 = 85</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The last one is the neatest, and the least known. A sum will tolerate you moving as much as you like from one number to the other, as long as you give exactly what you take. That lets you make one number round, and round numbers are free.</figcaption>
</figure>

The point is not to pick the "correct" one. The point is that there are several,
so you can take whichever suits the numbers in front of you. If one number is
near a ten, round. If both are far from one, go left to right.

## Larger numbers

The technique scales. The only difference is that you take one more digit group.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">468 + 275, left to right</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The hundreds</b><small>400 + 200 = 600. You now know the answer is "six hundred something" or "seven hundred something".</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The tens</b><small>60 + 70 = 130. Add it on: 600 + 130 = 730.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The units</b><small>8 + 5 = 13. Add it on: 730 + 13 = 743.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Note that none of the steps asks you to remember a carry. Each group is added straight onto the running total. What you hold in your head is always one number – not a number plus a carry plus where you were.</figcaption>
</figure>

And where numbers sit near something round, rounding is still fastest:

- **1,250 + 890** → 1,250 + 900 = 2,150, minus 10 = **2,140**
- **2,996 + 1,897** → 3,000 + 1,900 = 4,900, minus 4 and minus 3 = **4,893**

That last one looks like something you have to write down. Rounded, it is two
numbers that are easy to add and a correction of seven.

## Subtraction: you never need to borrow

This is the technique that changes the most, because borrowing is the part
people dislike.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Move both numbers by the same amount and the difference does not change.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>83 − 47:</b> add 3 to both. That gives <b>86 − 50</b>, which is 36. Done. No borrowing, because what you are taking away is now round.<br /><b>743 − 268:</b> add 32 to both. That gives <b>775 − 300 = 475</b>. It looks like a harder sum, but the 32 was chosen to turn 268 into 300 – and a round number comes away without resistance.</p>
</figure>

This is not a trick, it is what a difference is. The distance between two
numbers does not change if you slide both the same way. Think of a ruler you
push along: the gap between the marks stays put.

The other routes work on **83 − 47** too:

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">83 − 47 = 36</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Technique</th><th scope="col">How it goes</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Shift both</th><td>86 − 50 = 36</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Round and correct</th><td>83 − 50 = 33. You took 3 too many: 33 + 3 = 36</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Count up</th><td>From 47 to 50 is 3. From 50 to 83 is 33. Total 36</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Left to right</th><td>80 − 40 = 40, then 3 − 7 = −4. 40 − 4 = 36</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The bottom row is worth noticing: going left to right, a digit group is allowed to go negative. 3 − 7 = −4 is not a problem, it is just something you take away. The school method borrows in order to avoid negative numbers. In your head it is simpler to allow them.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Count up, not down

You probably do this already, without thinking of it as a technique.

When you get 53 back from a hundred, nobody worked out 100 − 47 in their head.
They counted upwards: from 47 to 50, and from 50 to 100.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">When the numbers sit close together</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>2025 − 1987</b><small>Taking away feels heavy. Counting up makes it trivial.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>To the nearest round number</b><small>From 1987 to 1990 is 3.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The rest of the way</b><small>From 1990 to 2025 is 35.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Add them</b><small>3 + 35 = 38.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The rule is simple: if the numbers are close together, count up. If they are far apart, take away. "How old is someone born in 1987" is a counting-up question, not a subtraction.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Round numbers, digit by digit

**1,000 − 376** has a rule of its own, and it is worth knowing by heart.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Subtracting from a round number of nothing but zeros, you turn each digit into "what is missing up to 9" – and the last one into "what is missing up to 10".</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>1,000 − 376:</b> 9 − 3 = 6, 9 − 7 = 2, 10 − 6 = 4. The answer is <b>624</b>.<br /><b>10,000 − 4,728:</b> 9 − 4 = 5, 9 − 7 = 2, 9 − 2 = 7, 10 − 8 = 2. The answer is <b>5,272</b>.<br />The rule works because 1,000 is 999 + 1. You subtract from 999 digit by digit, with no borrowing, and put the one back in the units place.</p>
</figure>

And where the number is not quite round, combine it with counting up:
**500 − 137** → from 137 to 140 is 3, from 140 to 500 is 360, giving **363**.

## The shopping basket

The most common real use of mental arithmetic is a row of numbers to be added
roughly, and preferably a little faster than the till.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">129 + 248 + 96</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Round everything up</b><small>130 + 250 + 100 = 480. These are numbers you add without thinking.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Keep track of what you added</b><small>1 on the first, 2 on the second, 4 on the third. Seven in total.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Subtract at the end</b><small>480 − 7 = 473.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The trick is that the roundings are kept separate. You add easy numbers and settle the bill once, at the end. If all you want is a rough answer you stop after the first step – and 480 is close enough to know whether you can afford it.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Checking the answer

Mental arithmetic without a check is guessing with confidence. The simplest
check is **casting out nines**, and it takes two seconds.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">Casting out nines on 468 + 275 = 743</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Add up the digits</b><small>4 + 6 + 8 = 18 → 1 + 8 = 9 → 0. And 2 + 7 + 5 = 14 → 1 + 4 = 5.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Do the sum with the remainders</b><small>0 + 5 = 5.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Compare with the answer</b><small>7 + 4 + 3 = 14 → 5. The same number, so the answer passes.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>You add the digits until one digit is left, treating 9 as 0. What you end up with is the remainder when the number is divided by 9 – and those remainders obey the same arithmetic as the numbers themselves.</figcaption>
</figure>

But it needs saying plainly what the check *cannot* do:

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Casting out nines catches most arithmetic slips, but never two digits swapping places.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>It works:</b> answer 753 instead of 743, and the digits give 6 against 5, so the error surfaces.<br /><b>It does not work:</b> answer 734, and the digits still give 5. Swapping two digits does not change the digit sum, so the check sees nothing. The same goes for being out by 9 or by 90. The check says "probably right", not "right".</p>
</figure>

## What actually needs practising

The techniques above are few, and take minutes to understand. What takes time is
two things, and they are worth drilling deliberately.

**Bonds to ten.** The pairs that make 10: 1 and 9, 2 and 8, 3 and 7, 4 and 6,
5 and 5. These have to come without a pause, because every rounding and every
count-up uses them.

**Two digits plus two digits.** Not because it is hard, but because it is the
building block. Once 47 + 38 is automatic, 468 + 275 is just the same sum done
once more.

The rest is choosing a technique. And that choice becomes automatic quickly,
because the numbers tell you what pays off: near a round number means round,
close together means count up, otherwise go left to right.
