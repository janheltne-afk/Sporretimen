A thorough walk through what actually affects your sleep – and what you can do about
it.

The starting point is personal: Jan Sindre struggled to fall asleep for a period,
read up on it, and worked methodically through the advice in Matthew Walker's book.
The solution turned out in the end to be something he had not considered at all.

The episode is built on what **Matthew Walker**, **Andrew Huberman** and **Bryan
Johnson** agree on – not on what they disagree about. Everything is explained with
the Norwegian conditions in mind: the dark season in winter and the bright nights in
summer.

## The two systems everything else rests on

Nearly every piece of sleep advice you hear works through one of two systems. Grasp
which one, and you also understand why the advice is given – and what happens when
you break it.

  <p class="fig__title">Circadian rhythm and sleep pressure</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">System 1</p>
      <h4>The circadian rhythm</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The body clock that tells you where in the day you are</li>
        <li>Set first and foremost by light</li>
        <li>Melatonin belongs here – the signal that evening has come</li>
        <li>That is why morning light works, and why a fixed wake-up time counts for more than a fixed bedtime</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">System 2</p>
      <h4>Sleep pressure</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>How long it is since you last slept</li>
        <li>Builds through the whole day, as adenosine</li>
        <li>Reset when you sleep – and partly by a long afternoon nap</li>
        <li>That is why caffeine works: it gets in the way of the adenosine signal without removing the pressure</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The two are independent of each other, and you sleep well when they pull the same way. Lying awake while exhausted is usually sleep pressure running high while the circadian rhythm still thinks it is daytime.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Why the afternoon coffee counts

Caffeine has a half-life of five to six hours. That does not mean the effect is over
by then – it means half of it is still there.

## The day that hangs together

<figure class="fig fig--cycle">
  <p class="fig__title">Four links, in order</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__step">1</span><b>A fixed wake-up time</b><small>The same hour every day, weekends included. This is the link that holds the rest in place.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">2</span><b>Light early</b><small>Get outside into daylight as early as you can. In winter even a grey day counts for more than indoor lighting does.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">3</span><b>A caffeine cut-off</b><small>Set a limit in the middle of the day and keep it. The difference shows up in the evening.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">4</span><b>An hour of winding down</b><small>Dimmed light, a lower gear, no screen right before. You do not go from a hundred to zero in a minute.</small></li>
  </ol>
  <p class="fig__loop">↻ And then it begins again the next morning</p>
  <figcaption>The links act on one another: get up at a fixed time and the morning light is easier to reach, and then the evening falls into place by itself. When one link goes, it is usually the first that has slipped.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Alcohol makes you fall asleep faster, but sleep worse.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Why this confuses people:</b> falling asleep is the part you notice. What happens through the rest of the night – broken sleep and less dream sleep – is the part you are not awake enough to register. That is why it feels as though it works.</p>
</figure>
