You have been in that conversation. Something felt wrong about what the other
person said, but you could not put your finger on what – and by the time you
worked it out in the car home, it was too late.

This episode is about being able to put your finger on it straight away.

## What a fallacy actually is

A **fallacy** is an error in the *argument*, not in the *conclusion*. It is a
piece of reasoning where the premises do not carry the claim, even though it
may sound entirely right.

That is an important distinction, and it is the first thing people get wrong.
Someone arguing terribly that the earth is round does not make the earth flat.
We will come back to that.

Traditionally a line is drawn between **formal** fallacies, where the structure
of the logic itself breaks, and **informal** ones, where the problem lies in
the content, the language or the context. The informal ones are what you meet
in practice, and they are what we spend the time on.

## The straw man

The most common trick of all. You build a weaker version of what the other
person said, knock it down, and act as though you have won.

> — I think the speed limit should be lowered in residential areas.
> — So you want everyone crawling along everywhere?

That is not what was said. And here is the point that often gets missed: the
straw man is usually not bad faith. We hear what we expect to hear, and build a
version of the other side that is easier to disagree with.

The counter-move is the **steelman**: repeat the other person's position in the
strongest version you can manage, and ask them to confirm you have understood
it. Do that, and you quite often find the disagreement is smaller than you
thought.

## Ad hominem – and when it is not a fallacy

Attacking the person instead of the argument:

> — You can't have a view on money, you went bankrupt yourself.

But you have to be precise here, because **ad hominem is not always a
fallacy.** It is one when the trait is irrelevant to the claim. When it is
relevant, it is a legitimate point:

- “He is wrong about climate because he is a bad father” – a fallacy.
- “The report was paid for by the industry it assesses” – *not* a fallacy. That
  is relevant information about credibility, and something any journalist is
  expected to disclose.

The line is whether the trait has anything to do with the matter at hand.

## The appeal to authority – which is usually fine

This is the most misused label on the list. People shout “appeal to authority”
whenever anyone cites an expert, and that is wrong.

Relying on relevant experts is **normal and sensible** thinking. You do not
calculate the load-bearing capacity of the bridge yourself. The fallacy arises
in three cases:

- **Wrong field.** A Nobel laureate in physics holding forth on nutrition.
- **No real authority.** Titles that sound weighty without meaning anything.
- **No such agreement.** One researcher presented as “the research shows”, when
  the field is divided.

Rule of thumb: check whether the person is an expert on *this specific thing*,
and whether they represent the breadth of the field or one extreme of it.

## The false dilemma

Two options presented as though they were the only ones.

> — Either we cut welfare, or the country goes bankrupt.

There is almost always a third, fourth and fifth possibility. The trick works
because two choices are easy to hold in your head, and because it pushes you to
pick a side before you have had a chance to ask whether there are more.

## The slippery slope – which often is not a fallacy

> — Allow this, and we will end up allowing anything.

Here is the nuance that is usually missing: **a slippery slope is only a
fallacy when the causal chain is not argued for.** If you can show that each
step actually follows from the previous one, it is a perfectly valid argument –
and that kind of reasoning is used daily in law, in safety work and in
politics.

The question is never “are they using a slippery slope?”, but “do the links
hold?”.

## The circular argument

The conclusion is already contained in the premise.

> — Why should we trust him? Because he is trustworthy.

Longer versions are harder to spot, because the circle can run through several
paragraphs before it returns to where it started.

## Shifting the burden of proof

> — Can you prove it *doesn't* exist?

The burden of proof sits with whoever makes the claim. You owe nobody a proof
that something does not exist – and in most cases it is not possible anyway.

## Whataboutism

Answering criticism by pointing at something else.

> — You delivered late.
> — Yes, but you did the same last year.

That may well be true. It is simply not an answer. Notice that it often works:
the conversation moves, and the original question is never addressed.

## No true Scotsman

A counter-example is defined away instead of being taken seriously.

> — No Norwegian skips the Sunday walk.
> — My neighbour does.
> — Then he is no true Norwegian.

The rule is rescued by changing what it is about, mid-argument.

## Appeals to nature and to tradition

> — It is natural, therefore it is good.
> — We have always done it this way.

That something is natural says nothing about whether it is good – arsenic is
natural, and vaccines are not. That something is old says nothing about whether
it works. Both can be useful *indications*, but neither is an argument.

## Hasty generalisation, and correlation that is not cause

Two that belong together.

**Hasty generalisation** is drawing a rule from too few cases: two bad
experiences with a brand, and the brand is bad. **Correlation is not
causation** is when two things track each other without either causing the
other. Ice cream sales and drownings track each other closely. The cause is
summer.

The third variant is **after, therefore because of**: something happened after
something else, so the first must have caused the second.

## The Gish gallop and motte-and-bailey

Two tricks you notice most in online debate.

The **Gish gallop** is firing off so many claims in so little time that the
other side cannot possibly answer them. It takes twenty seconds to assert
something and ten minutes to refute it, and that asymmetry can be exploited
deliberately.

**Motte-and-bailey** is alternating between two versions of the same position:
a bold one that is hard to defend (the bailey), and a cautious one almost
nobody disagrees with (the motte). Attack the bold version, and the arguer
retreats to the cautious one – and once the attack is over, they are back in
the bailey.

## The fallacy fallacy

The most important one, and the one that makes this whole list dangerous to
learn.

**A bad argument does not make the conclusion false.** If someone uses a straw
man to defend a position, the position may still be right – they just have not
shown it yet.

An honest warning: people who have just learned about fallacies often become
unbearable. They start shouting Latin names instead of answering the point, and
use the list as a weapon rather than a tool. That is using the knowledge
backwards.

The most useful thing is not catching other people. It is noticing when you are
doing it yourself – because a straw man is far easier to build than to spot,
and nearly all of us build them without meaning anything by it.

## How to actually respond

Four moves that work better than quoting Latin:

1. **Repeat the position back.** “As I understand you, you think …” If it was a
   straw man, it is cleared up immediately.
2. **Ask for the missing link.** “How does the second thing follow from the
   first?” Far more effective than giving the trick a name.
3. **Hold on to one question.** Against whataboutism and the gallop: “We can
   come to that – first I would like an answer to this.”
4. **Separate the matter from the person.** Including when you would rather
   not.
