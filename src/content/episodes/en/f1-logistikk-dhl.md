A Formula 1 race lasts under two hours. Getting the equipment there takes months,
and it happens more than twenty times a year, on five continents.

That makes the sport one of the most demanding logistics problems in practice –
not because the volume is large, it is modest next to ordinary freight, but
because the time window is absolute and the equipment is irreplaceable. If it
arrives two days late, there is no replacement to buy.

Formula 1 has described one pack-up in detail, and the figures give a good sense
of the order of magnitude.

## The sea freight kits

Here comes the cleverest move in the whole system, and one that transfers far
beyond motorsport.

The problem is obvious: sea freight takes weeks. A race in Australia and one in
Bahrain can be two weeks apart. A single container never makes it home and out
again.

The solution is not to ship faster. The solution is to **buy your way out of the
return problem**.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">How the kits leapfrog each other</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Several complete sets</b><small>Each team has several identical sets of sea freight equipment – garage, kitchen, furniture – rather than one that is moved around.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Each set ships far in advance</b><small>The equipment for the season opener in Australia left the United Kingdom as early as 7 January.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The sets run ahead in the calendar</b><small>While one set is in use at one race, the next ones are already on their way to later races.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Nobody waits for the return</b><small>A used set can take months to come home without holding anything up, because it is not needed again until much later.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>This is a classic trade-off between tied-up capital and speed, and the answer is the opposite of what lean thinking would suggest: instead of reducing inventory, you sharply increase the number of copies. It pays because the alternative – flying kitchen equipment and garage panelling – is dramatically more expensive than owning five kitchens.</figcaption>
</figure>

It is worth stating what this costs: teams tie up capital in equipment that sits
unused most of the year. It is accepted because air freight is so much more
expensive per kilo that duplication becomes the cheaper option.

## The air freight

What must fly, flies – and it is done by charter, not by ordinary scheduled
freight.

The reason is not capacity but control. Scheduled aircraft go when they go, and
cargo can be bumped. A charter goes when the team needs it, and the cargo is the
team's own.

DHL, the sport's logistics partner, states that it puts several freighters into
service per overseas race, and that the number rises when the support series F2
and F3 race the same weekend. Total volume for the whole field per event is given
as being in the order of over a thousand tonnes, but the figure varies between
seasons and sources, so it should be read as an order of magnitude rather than a
precise number.

## The time window

The asymmetry here is what makes the sport distinctive as a logistics case.

      <h4>Days</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Planned well in advance</li>
        <li>Happens in familiar surroundings</li>
        <li>All equipment is to hand</li>

    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Pack-up after the flag</p>
      <h4>Hours</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Starts the moment the race ends</li>
        <li>Happens at an unfamiliar circuit</li>
        <li>Every team packs up at once</li>
        <li>The aircraft leaves regardless</li>

    </div>

  <figcaption>It is the right-hand column that shapes the whole design. When pack-up has to run in hours, packing cannot be a problem solved on the spot. It has to be <em>predetermined</em>: every item has its place in its box, and the boxes have their place on the pallet. That is the same logic behind standardised work in lean – not to save seconds, but to remove the need to think under pressure.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Weight

In sea freight you pay for space. In air freight you effectively pay for weight,
and that makes weight an engineering problem in itself – not only on the car, but
on the boxes around it.

Formula 1 has therefore developed its own lighter transport cases. According to
the sport itself, a composite material with a polypropylene honeycomb structure
has been used, the cases also serve as protection for broadcast equipment, they
are reused at the circuit rather than standing empty, and the weight is said to be
reduced by around 60 per cent against what they replaced.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">A case that weighs less is cheaper to move every single time it moves.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Why it compounds so hard:</b> the packaging is flown trip after trip, season after season. A weight saving is not repaid once, but on every flight in the case's lifetime.<br /><b>Why it is also reused at the circuit:</b> a case that becomes something useful when empty replaces equipment that would otherwise have had to be shipped as well.<br /><b>The transferable lesson:</b> this holds for all repeating transport. If the packaging is heavy and empty on the return leg, that is usually where the largest untapped saving sits.</p>
</figure>

## The calendar

Finally, the factor that decides most, and that is settled by people who are not
in logistics.

## What is worth taking with you

That a team moves around 42 tonnes to an overseas race, and that only part of it
goes by air.

That the line runs not between important and unimportant, but between what must be
that exact object and what merely has to be identical.

That several complete sea freight kits are a way of buying your way out of the
return problem – expensive in capital, cheap against the alternative.

That packing takes days and pack-up takes hours, and that it is the latter that
shapes the solution.

That weight in air freight is paid on every single trip, which makes the packaging
worth engineering.

And that the calendar decides more of the cost than any single logistics decision
does.
