TEN-T is often described as "the EU's train project". That is the most common way
of talking about it in Norwegian public debate, and it is a useful way in – but it
is not what TEN-T is.

The network does cover rail. But it also covers roads, ports, inland waterways,
short sea shipping, airports and terminals, with cities as the nodes. Rail gets
most of the attention because that is where the requirements bite hardest and the
backlog is largest, but it is one part among several.

This episode goes through what the network actually is, which deadlines apply,
what the 2024 regulation requires – and how Norway fits into all of it.

## What TEN-T is

TEN-T stands for Trans-European Transport Network. It is the EU's framework for
building one coherent transport network across national borders, instead of
twenty-seven national networks that meet at the frontier by coincidence.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">What the network covers</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Part</th><th scope="col">What it means in practice</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Rail</th><td>Both passenger and freight. This is where the hardest requirements sit.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Roads</th><td>The main roads tying the nodes together.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Inland waterways</th><td>Rivers and canals, a far larger mode on the continent than in Norway.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Short sea shipping</th><td>The sea route as an alternative to hauling goods the long way round by land.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Ports</th><td>Both maritime and inland ports.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Airports</th><td>With their own requirements on how they connect to rail.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Terminals</th><td>Where goods change mode. Often the real bottleneck.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The point of listing it all out is that TEN-T is a <em>multimodal</em> framework. The value lies not in each individual part but in the transitions between them – and it is precisely those transitions that are hardest to standardise, because they require two different industries to agree.</figcaption>
</figure>

The underlying idea is the same as with the barcode: the valuable thing is not the
technology but the agreement that everyone does it the same way. A railway line
that stops at the border because the signalling system on the other side is a
different one is not half a line. It is a dead end.

## Three layers, three deadlines

This is the structure you need in your head to read news about TEN-T. The network
is divided into three layers, and each layer has its own completion deadline.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">The layers, and when they are to be complete</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The core network – 2030</b><small>The most important connections between major cities and nodes. This is where the requirements bite first.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The extended core network – 2040</b><small>An intermediate layer introduced by the 2024 revision, with a deadline ten years later than the core.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The comprehensive network – 2050</b><small>Connects every region of the EU to the core network. The largest layer, and the one with the longest horizon.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The extended core network is the most significant change in the revision. It creates a middle step between "most important" and "everything else", so that routes that matter a great deal without being absolutely central get a real deadline instead of landing in the 2050 pile. So when someone says TEN-T "has to be finished by 2030", that applies only to the innermost layer.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The nine corridors

On top of the layers sit the corridors: nine named axes capturing the largest
long-distance flows. The 2024 revision merged two schemes that previously ran side
by side – the core network corridors and the rail freight corridors – into what
are now called **European Transport Corridors**.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">The nine corridors</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Corridor</th></tr></thead>

      <tr><td>Baltic Sea–Adriatic Sea</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Mediterranean</td></tr>
      <tr><td>North Sea–Baltic</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Baltic Sea–Black Sea–Aegean Sea</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Western Balkans–Eastern Mediterranean</td></tr>
      <tr><td>North Sea–Rhine–Mediterranean</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Rhine–Danube</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Scandinavian–Mediterranean</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The last one is the one that matters most to Norway. A corridor is defined by crossing at least two borders and involving at least three transport modes – that definition is what makes them multimodal, and what separates them from a plain railway line on a map.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What the regulation actually requires

This is where it becomes concrete, and these are the numbers that recur in news
coverage without anyone explaining them.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Four requirements from the regulation</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Requirement</th><th scope="col">What it involves</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">160 km/h</th><td>Passenger lines on the core and extended core network must support at least 160 km/h by 2040.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">ERTMS</th><td>The common European signalling system is to be deployed across the whole network.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">740 metres</th><td>Terminals must have capacity for freight trains of 740 metres.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Airport connection</th><td>Airports with more than 12 million passengers a year must be connected to long-distance rail.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Note that none of the four is about building new lines. They are about making existing infrastructure compatible. That is a far less glamorous kind of policy than new high-speed lines, and probably a more consequential one.</figcaption>
</figure>

## ERTMS: one signalling system instead of many

This is the part that is hardest to grasp from the outside, and the one that
matters most.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">A train crossing a border has historically met a different signalling system on the other side.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>What it costs in practice:</b> the locomotive must either carry several systems on board, or be swapped at the border. The latter takes time, requires staff, and turns the border crossing into a fixed cost in every single service.<br /><b>What ERTMS does:</b> replaces the national systems with one common one. A locomotive with ERTMS can in principle run through the whole network without changing system.<br /><b>Why it takes time:</b> signalling installations have long lifespans, the rollout has to happen while trains are still running, and both the infrastructure and every individual locomotive must be rebuilt. This is not a software update.</p>
</figure>

Norway is worth mentioning here, because we have our own ERTMS programme running
independently of the EU deadlines. So this is not a requirement Norway meets
unprepared – the work was under way long before the 2024 regulation.

## Why 740 metres

The number looks arbitrary. It is not. It is a capacity requirement, not a speed
requirement.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">What train length actually decides</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Short trains</p>
      <h4>More of everything per tonne</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>One driver per train, whatever the length</li>
        <li>One path in the timetable per train</li>
        <li>More departures for the same volume of goods</li>
        <li>Weaker competition against lorries</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">740 metres</p>
      <h4>Same input, more goods</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Same driver, same path</li>
        <li>Fewer departures for the same volume</li>
        <li>Lower cost per tonne</li>
        <li>But: requires the <em>whole</em> route to handle it</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>That last point is the whole reason the requirement sits in a regulation rather than in a national plan. A 740-metre train can only run a route where every terminal, passing loop and loading area is long enough. If one link is too short, the entire route is limited to that link. Capacity is set by the weakest part, not by the average.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Norway

Norway is not an EU member, so the question people ask is a fair one: are we
actually part of this?

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">What Norway is part of, and what it is not</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">In</p>
      <h4>The rules</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The regulation was incorporated into the EEA Agreement in October 2025</li>
        <li>Norwegian infrastructure appears on the maps</li>
        <li>The Scandinavian–Mediterranean corridor extends north to here</li>
        <li>Norway has taken part in TEN-T cooperation since the 1990s</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Out</p>
      <h4>The money</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The funding instrument (CEF) is not part of the EEA Agreement</li>
        <li>Norway does not take part in TEN-T funding</li>
        <li>Projects must therefore be financed from national budgets</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>This is the part worth taking with you. Norway is on the map and follows the rules, but sits outside the fund that pays for construction elsewhere in the network. The Norwegian government describes the cooperation as voluntary cooperation under Protocol 31 of the EEA Agreement, that is, outside the ordinary transport provisions.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What is worth being sceptical about

Three things, which apply to most large infrastructure plans.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Three caveats</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Deadlines are not completion</b><small>2030 is an obligation in a regulation, not a forecast. Large infrastructure projects run late almost everywhere, and TEN-T is no exception.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Maps are not money</b><small>A route being drawn into the network does not mean it is funded. It means it qualifies to be.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The weakest part decides</b><small>For train length, signalling and speed alike, the benefit only arrives once the whole route is upgraded. A corridor that is 90 per cent complete does not deliver 90 per cent of the benefit.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The last is the most useful rule of thumb when reading about projects of this kind. Benefit in a network does not arrive gradually, in step with how much has been built. It arrives in jumps, when the last gap on a route is closed.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What is worth taking with you

That TEN-T is not a railway project but a multimodal framework in which rail is
simply the part with the hardest requirements.

That the network has three layers with their own deadlines – 2030, 2040 and 2050 –
and that "finished by 2030" applies only to the innermost one.

That the requirements are about compatibility rather than new construction: a
common signalling system, a common train length, a speed threshold.

And that Norway is part of the rules, but not of the funding.

*The TEN-T names and corridor designations belong to the EU. This episode explains
how the framework is built and does not reproduce the text of the regulation. For
binding detail, Regulation (EU) 2024/1679 and the Norwegian implementing decisions
apply.*
