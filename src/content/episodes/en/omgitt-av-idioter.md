**Thomas Erikson** published *Surrounded by Idiots* in 2014, and it has since
sold in the millions worldwide. The founding idea is that people can be sorted
into four types, and that nearly all irritation between people comes from
talking past one another.

This is an episode about a book many people have read – and about why the
question in the title has a fairly clear answer.

## The four colours

- **Red.** Fast, direct, results-oriented. Wants the conclusion first and
  handles conflict well.
- **Yellow.** Outgoing, enthusiastic, talkative. Motivated by attention and new
  ideas, less by detail.
- **Green.** Calm, loyal, cooperative. Dislikes abrupt change and wants everyone
  to be comfortable.
- **Blue.** Analytical, precise, cautious. Wants the numbers, and wants them
  right.

Practically, the book walks through how to talk to each colour: short and direct
to red, listen and let yellow talk, do not surprise green, and give blue the
documentation.

## Where the model comes from

Erikson uses **DISA**, a variant of what is internationally called **DISC**. It
traces back to the psychologist William Marston's book *Emotions of Normal
People* from 1928 – and Marston was also the man behind the early lie detector
and the Wonder Woman comics.

It is worth noting that Marston never developed a test. The DISC instruments came
later, from others, and the framework itself is nearly a century old and sits
outside modern personality psychology.

## Why it feels so accurate

This is the interesting question. Almost everyone who reads the book recognises
themselves, and recognises their colleagues. That is real – but it is not
evidence.

The explanation is the **Barnum effect**: we experience vague, generally
positive descriptions as precisely accurate about ourselves. It is the same
mechanism that makes horoscopes work, and in classic experiments people
recognise themselves in a personality profile even when every participant
receives the *same* text.

Add that four categories are few enough to remember, and that the descriptions
are written to cover most of the range, and you have a model it is nearly
impossible not to recognise yourself in.

## What the field uses instead

Personality psychology has settled on the **five-factor model** – often called
the Big Five: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and
neuroticism.

The decisive difference is not the number. It is that the five-factor model
measures **degrees** on five scales, not **types**. People are distributed along
continuums with most in the middle, not in four boxes. Saying someone “is red” is
rather like dividing the population into “tall” and “short” and treating them as
two species.

## The criticism

It should be stated plainly. In 2018 the Swedish association **Vetenskap och
Folkbildning** gave Erikson its **Årets förvillare** award – Misleader of the
Year – for spreading a personality model without scientific support, and for
presenting it as though it had some. Several psychologists have also pointed out
that he is described as a behavioural expert without formal training in
psychology.

The objections to DISC as an instrument are also well documented: weak
test–retest reliability, unclear predictive validity, and almost no relationship
with job performance. That last point is worth carrying away for anyone who has
been assigned a colour at a work seminar.

## What you can still take from it

Not a model of what people *are*. But the book carries a simple and useful
reminder: people want to be spoken to in different ways, and what you
experience as clear communication, others experience as brusque, imprecise or
fussy. That insight needs no colours – but some people reach it more easily with
them.

*The content is a walk through what the book argues, together with the criticism
it has met. Not a personality instrument, and no substitute for professional
assessment.*
