**Nassim Nicholas Taleb** was an options trader before he was an author, and
*The Black Swan* (2007) arrived the year before the financial crisis – which did
the sales no harm.

A **black swan** has three marks: it is extremely rare, it has enormous
consequences, and it appears obvious in hindsight. The name comes from the fact
that Europeans held for centuries that all swans were white, until someone
reached Australia. A thousand confirmations became worthless in a moment.

## Two kinds of randomness

Taleb's most useful distinction. In **Mediocristan**, things follow the normal
distribution: height, weight, calorie intake. Add the tallest human alive to a
sample of a thousand and the average barely moves.

In **Extremistan**, that does not hold. Wealth, book sales, city size, returns,
epidemics. Add Jeff Bezos to the same sample and the average is useless. Here a
handful of events account for nearly the whole result.

The mistake, Taleb argues, is that we use Mediocristan's tools – standard
deviation, the normal distribution, risk models – on things that live in
Extremistan. That produces a false confidence worse than no model at all.

## The turkey problem

A turkey is fed every day for a thousand days. With each day it has more
statistical evidence that humans are benevolent. Its confidence peaks the day
before Thanksgiving.

The point is the limit of induction: that something has gone well for a long
time is not evidence it will continue. Often it is the opposite – long
stability can mean risk has been quietly accumulating.

## Why we think we could have seen it

- **The narrative fallacy.** We cannot tolerate randomness, so we manufacture
  causes after the fact. After a market crash there is always an explanation on
  the evening news, and it did not exist that morning.
- **Silent evidence.** We study those who succeeded, and never see the graveyard
  of those who did the same and vanished. That makes us systematically
  overestimate how well recipes work.
- **The ludic fallacy.** Dice and roulette have known probabilities. Reality
  does not, and using the casino as a model for the world confuses risk with
  uncertainty.

## What to do in practice

Taleb is clear that the answer is not to become a better forecaster. It is to
arrange things so you can **survive** the unforeseen – and ideally profit from
it:

- Avoid situations where a single event can take you out.
- Buffers and generous margins, even when they look wasteful.
- Be careful with debt and leverage, which remove the slack.
- Expose yourself to upside with limited downside, not the reverse.

## The criticism

The book is polemical, repetitive and generous with contempt for economists,
forecasters and other authors. That makes it tiring, and makes it easy to
dismiss a point that is actually good.

Substantively, several people have noted that the ideas are not as new as the
presentation suggests – Frank Knight distinguished risk from uncertainty in
1921, and Keynes wrote about the same thing. The concept is also nearly
unfalsifiable as a claim: if something unexpected happens, it was a black swan;
if it does not, we are still waiting. And it has been applied to anything that
surprises anyone, which infuriates Taleb himself.

What holds up is the distinction between the two kinds of randomness, and the
advice to build to survive rather than to build to be right.
