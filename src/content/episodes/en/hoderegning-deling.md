Of the four operations, division is the one people name as the worst. It is
worth asking why, because the answer points straight at the fix.

The reason is that the method we were taught – long division, numbers stacked
up, remainders written underneath – was designed for paper. It keeps track of
several things at once by writing them down. Try to run it in your head and you
have to remember everything the paper would otherwise be holding.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Division in your head is not done by calculating faster. It is done by turning the sum into something else.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Three detours, and they cover almost everything:</b> split the <i>divisor</i> into factors, so one hard division becomes two easy ones. Split the <i>number</i> into pieces that are each easy to divide. Or turn the division into a <i>multiplication</i>. Which one you pick is decided by the numbers – and after a little practice you see it at once.</p>
</figure>

## Split the divisor into factors

This is the most usable technique of them all, and it is close to free.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Two steps instead of one</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Dividing by</th><th scope="col">Do this instead</th><th scope="col">Example</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">4</th><td>halve twice</td><td>1,236 → 618 → 309</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">6</th><td>halve, then divide by 3</td><td>2,814 → 1,407 → 469</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">8</th><td>halve three times</td><td>216 → 108 → 54 → 27</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">9</th><td>divide by 3, then by 3 again</td><td>1,638 → 546 → 182</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">12</th><td>divide by 4, then by 3</td><td>1,008 → 252 → 84</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">24</th><td>divide by 8, then by 3</td><td>552 → 69 → 23</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Halving is the one operation almost everybody does without effort, which is why 4 and 8 are among the easiest numbers to divide by – not the hardest. 216 divided by 8 looks like a sum you have to write down. As three halvings it is barely a calculation.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Multiply instead of dividing

Some divisors have a multiplying partner that does the work, because they go
into 100.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Divide by multiplying, then move the point</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Dividing by</th><th scope="col">Multiply by</th><th scope="col">And divide by</th><th scope="col">Example</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">5</th><td>2</td><td>10</td><td>435 → 870 → 87</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">25</th><td>4</td><td>100</td><td>1,700 → 6,800 → 68</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">50</th><td>2</td><td>100</td><td>3,150 → 6,300 → 63</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">500</th><td>2</td><td>1,000</td><td>the same move, one zero further</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>It works because 5 is 10 divided by 2, and 25 is 100 divided by 4. Dividing by 5 is therefore dividing by 10 and multiplying by 2 – and the order is free, so you can do the multiplication first while the number is still whole. Moving the decimal point is not arithmetic at all.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Split the number

Where the divisor will not simplify, the number often will.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">1,236 ÷ 4</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Find a friendly piece</b><small>1,200 divides by 4, and it is easy to see: 12 ÷ 4 = 3, so 1,200 ÷ 4 = 300.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Take the rest separately</b><small>What is left is 36, and 36 ÷ 4 = 9.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Add the answers</b><small>300 + 9 = 309.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>This is the same property that makes multiplication easier to split: division distributes over addition. The trick is to choose the split to suit the divisor, not according to where the number "naturally" breaks. Dividing by 7, you look for pieces like 700 and 70 – not for hundreds and tens.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Short division

When nothing will simplify, there is still a version of the school method that
genuinely works in your head. It is called short division, and the difference is
that you hold only one remainder at a time.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">3,458 ÷ 7</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>34 ÷ 7</b><small>Four times, remainder 6. Note 4 as the first digit and carry the remainder.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>65 ÷ 7</b><small>The remainder 6 goes in front of the next digit, 5. Nine times, remainder 2. Second digit is 9.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>28 ÷ 7</b><small>The remainder 2 in front of the last digit, 8. Four times, no remainder. Last digit is 4.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The answer</b><small>The digits in the order they came: 494.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>What you hold in your head is never more than two things: the digit you just found, and the remainder. The answer builds from the left, in the same order you say it. If you want to check it, 7 × 494 is a multiplication – and a multiplication is easier to check than a division.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Does it come out even?

Often the question is not what the answer is, but whether the division comes out
at all. You can see that from the digits, without calculating.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">The divisibility rules</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">A number divides by</th><th scope="col">If</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">2</th><td>the last digit is 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">3</th><td>the digit sum divides by 3</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">4</th><td>the last two digits form a number divisible by 4</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">5</th><td>the last digit is 0 or 5</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">6</th><td>the rules for both 2 and 3 hold</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">8</th><td>the last three digits form a number divisible by 8</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">9</th><td>the digit sum divides by 9</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">10</th><td>the last digit is 0</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The rules for 4 and 8 look odd, but follow from 100 dividing by 4 and 1,000 dividing by 8. Everything in front of those last digits is therefore already divisible, and only the tail decides. The rules for 3 and 9 follow from 10 being 9 + 1 – which is why it is the digits, and not their places, that matter.</figcaption>
</figure>

The two hardest numbers have rules too, and they are less well known:

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">The rule for 7: drop the last digit, and subtract twice that digit from what is left. If the result divides by 7, so does the number.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>203:</b> drop the 3 and subtract 6. That gives 20 − 6 = <b>14</b>, which is 7 × 2. So 203 divides by 7 – and 203 ÷ 7 = 29.<br /><b>1,547:</b> drop the 7 and subtract 14. That gives 154 − 14 = <b>140</b>, which is plainly divisible by 7. And 1,547 ÷ 7 = 221.<br />If the result is still too large to judge, apply the same move to it again.</p>
</figure>

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">The rule for 11: add the digits alternately plus and minus. If the total is 0, or a number divisible by 11, so is the number.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>8,294:</b> work from the right with alternating signs: 4 − 9 + 2 − 8 = <b>−11</b>. Divisible by 11, and 8,294 ÷ 11 = 754.<br /><b>3,729:</b> 9 − 2 + 7 − 3 = <b>11</b>. Also divisible: 3,729 ÷ 11 = 339.<br />The rule works because 10 is 11 − 1: every place you move to the left, the sign of what is left over flips.</p>
</figure>

## When it does not come out even

The commonest case in practice is that the answer is not a whole number. The
move then is to find the nearest thing you can, and handle the rest separately.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">1,000 ÷ 16</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Aim upwards with a multiplication</b><small>16 × 60 = 960. That is under 1,000, and close.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>See what is left</b><small>1,000 − 960 = 40.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Divide the rest</b><small>40 ÷ 16 is 2.5 – because 16 × 2 = 32 and half of 16 is 8.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Add them</b><small>60 + 2.5 = 62.5.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>This is division done as multiplication: you look for a number that, times 16, lands close, and correct afterwards. The advantage is that for most people the times tables sit better than division does. If all you need is an approximate answer, you stop after the first step.</figcaption>
</figure>

And where the answer runs forever, it helps to recognise it: **100 ÷ 7** is 14
with 2 left over, so a little over 14. Sevenths have decimals that never end,
and then "14 and a bit" is often the right answer to give.

## The fractions worth knowing

Half the division people do in daily life is really a percentage question. Know
these, and you skip the arithmetic.

## What actually needs practising

Three things, and all of them are small.

**Halving.** Of all the operations this is the one that repays most, because it
covers 2, 4, 8, 16 and half of percentage work. Practise on numbers that are not
obvious: 74, 178, 1,250.

**The times tables backwards.** Division is a multiplication you already know
the answer to. Faced with 56 and asked to divide by 7, the question is "what
times 7 makes 56" – and that is the times tables, asked from the other side.

**The rules for 3 and 9.** These are the two you will actually use, because they
look at the digits rather than the number, and take under a second.

The rest is recognising which detour fits. It is the same skill as in addition
and subtraction: what makes mental arithmetic easy is not speed, it is seeing
what the sum can be turned into.
