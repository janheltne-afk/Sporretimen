What does it actually involve to work as a paramedic? Håvard Hasund has several years
behind him in the Oslo ambulance service, and now works in a rural district and at an
out-of-hours clinic. That makes him one of few who can genuinely compare the two.

## It is not called an ambulance driver

That is one of the first things he corrects. "Ambulance driver" is the phrase most
people use, but it does not describe the job.

## How he got there

No plan, really. Håvard describes himself as the outdoors person in the class, not the
one heading into healthcare. At upper secondary the plan was to coast through and do as
little as possible.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Four steps he did not plan</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The Red Cross</b><small>Active towards the end of upper secondary. This is where the interest in first aid and emergency medicine was sparked – before that he had only done CPR on a dummy at school.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>An exchange year in Canada</b><small>To French-speaking Canada. He learned French, but repeated the school year at home – he had not learned much academically. What he did get was friends he still has.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The Navy</b><small>Recruit school, then the Coast Guard as a smoke diver. Regular first-aid training on board, some of it with a doctor. This is where the thought first surfaced.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The paramedic degree</b><small>Applied in 2019, and describes himself as lucky to have applied precisely then.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>He was a smoke diver – so why not the fire service? The answer is disarmingly honest: the heat. On the smoke-diving course he was in rooms with gas flames where it easily reaches several hundred degrees, and in the engine room on board it was 40–60. Cold is fine. Heat is not.</figcaption>
</figure>

## How he learned anatomy

This is perhaps the most reusable piece of advice in the episode, and it applies well
beyond medicine.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">I did not try to memorise. I tried to understand how things work in the body.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>The method:</b> he found he learned best from video, and used a lot of free lectures on YouTube where somebody explains and draws as they go – alongside reading the book and writing notes.<br /><b>The point:</b> once you understand how the systems connect, the exam becomes considerably easier than if you have memorised isolated facts.<br /><b>The effort:</b> he had lectures around three days a week, but treated the degree as a nine-to-five job five days a week, with reading in the evenings on top.</p>
</figure>

## When he considered quitting

In the spring of the second year came 16 weeks of placement. He describes the first
period honestly as awful.

The explanation is interesting. At college students are drilled in acute conditions,
which builds an expectation that the job is to arrive, find something acute, fix it and
drive to hospital. The first call-out was a young woman with a fever. Håvard examined
her, found nothing to act on, and was left with one question: what do I do now? His
supervisor calmly said they were driving to the GP.

Other call-outs went the other way – patients far sicker than he had the knowledge to
handle.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">A first placement is not about being world class. It is about functioning, staying humble and not being professionally lost.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>It was the supervisor who said it</b>, and Håvard is clear that a poor supervisor could easily have ended it differently – he thought many times that he was not suited to this.<br /><b>In the second period came another realisation:</b> the more you learn, the less certain you become. And for him, performance and confidence are closely linked.<br /><b>Then it clicked:</b> "Eventually it loosens up a bit, and you just start doing things. You stop overthinking."</p>
</figure>

As a student you do not drive. You are in the back with the patient, both ways. This was
also during the strictest infection-control period: full kit, a sealed suit, 30 degrees
and air conditioning that did not always work.

## Debriefing after difficult call-outs

This is one of the most important subjects in the episode. Håvard says there used to be
no debriefing system in the emergency services, and that the suicide rate among people
working in them was high compared with the rest of the population.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">How the scheme worked where he worked</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>You get a call</b><small>After a difficult incident.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>A couple of days later</b><small>Deliberately not immediately, so it is not completely raw.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>A conversation if needed</b><small>If the incident is heavy, or you feel the need, you are called in – preferably with everyone who was involved.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Confidential, and paid</b><small>Everything said is confidential, and attendance is paid as overtime.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>What makes the scheme good, he thinks, is who leads the conversations: not psychologists, but people who have worked in the ambulance service for years and have training in this. They have stood in comparable situations themselves. He is careful to say he does not know how it is organised elsewhere. Asked whether you become hardened, the answer is nuanced: somewhat – but a day comes when something reaches you, because it resembles your own life.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The dissertation: delirium in older patients

Håvard wrote about delirium and a screening tool for catching it early.

Briefly, as explained in the episode: older patients can develop a temporary state of
confusion when the body is placed under strain, for instance by an infection. It does
not mean the person is cognitively impaired ordinarily. It broadly appears in two forms
– **agitated**, restless and irritable, or **quiet**, where the patient is drowsy and
falls asleep without stimulus or mid-conversation. Older patients with a hip fracture
are particularly exposed, because the fracture is a major trauma for the body.

## Working in Oslo

He stayed in Oslo for six years, three as a student and three working, employed in the
staffing pool – a separate unit that fills gaps where there is absence or a need for
extra capacity. A typical day: turn up at a fixed place, change, have a coffee, and wait
for the staffing coordinator to ring and say which station you are going to.

In central Oslo there is a lot of overdose work, and at night a lot of drunkenness.
Violence and shootings occur. But he stresses something important: fundamentally it is
the same job across the country – a great many older people with heart problems and
COPD, wherever you are.

## The year on dispatch

He also worked a year split fifty-fifty: half as a medical dispatcher, half in the
ambulance. The reason was frustration. They went on many call-outs where they arrived
and found nothing, and it felt like resources spent for no reason. Rather than walk
around irritated at dispatch, he applied to go there and find out how it actually is.

## Who the job suits

His answer is unceremonious but apt. If you are interested in emergency medicine, this
is a good place to be. And you should like people – there is a lot of talking, and most
call-outs are transport or low-acuity situations rather than drama.

> It is a really enjoyable and rewarding job. You feel that you contribute – maybe not
> to people getting well, but at least to them having a decent trip and feeling they are
> well looked after.

*Emergency medicine is discussed at a general level and is not medical advice. No
identifiable patient stories are shared. Entry requirements change – always check
current information with the admissions service. In an emergency, call the emergency
number.*
