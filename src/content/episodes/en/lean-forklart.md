“Lean” is used today for almost anything meant to become more efficient, and
the word has lost much of its meaning along the way. To a good many people in
working life it sounds like a friendlier word for cutting staff.

That is not what the system was about. This episode goes back to what was
actually built, why it was built that way, which parts travel to other
industries – and which criticisms land.

## Where it comes from

Lean is a Western umbrella term for the **Toyota Production System**, developed
at Toyota in Japan in the decades after the Second World War. **Taiichi Ohno**
is regarded as its principal architect, with **Shigeo Shingo** as a central
contributor.

The starting point was scarcity. Japanese industry had little capital, little
space and few raw materials, and could not copy American mass production with
its large inventories and long runs. The system that grew out of that was an
answer to shortage – and that is worth keeping in mind, because it explains why
it puts so much weight on not making anything before it is needed.

The word “lean” itself came much later, and not from Toyota. **John Krafcik**
used it in an article in 1988, while attached to the IMVP research programme at
MIT. Two years later came **The Machine That Changed the World**, by Womack,
Jones and Roos, which carried the term far beyond the car industry.

## Value, and everything else

The whole system rests on one distinction, and it is worth taking seriously
before moving on to the tools.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Value is what the customer is actually willing to pay for. Everything else is waste.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>The test:</b> would the customer pay more for this step if they saw it? Welding the frame – yes. The part sitting in a warehouse for three weeks first, being carted back and forth between two buildings, someone filling in a form nobody reads – no. That does not make those steps unnecessary today, but it does make them candidates for removal.</p>
</figure>

Note the wording. Waste is not the same as unnecessary work. Much of the waste
in an organisation is work that *has* to be done given how the system is set up
right now. The point is to change the system, not to ask people to run faster.

## The seven wastes

Ohno set out seven kinds of waste. They were conceived for a factory, but they
translate to office work surprisingly directly.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">The seven wastes</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Waste</th><th scope="col">On a shop floor</th><th scope="col">In an office</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Overproduction</th><td>Making more than the next step needs now</td><td>Reports nobody reads, meetings nobody needed</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Waiting</th><td>A machine idle, an operator waiting for parts</td><td>Waiting for an approval, a reply, an access right</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Transport</th><td>Parts moved between buildings and halls</td><td>A case passed between four departments</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Overprocessing</th><td>Tighter tolerances than the customer needs</td><td>Fifteen fields where five would do</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Inventory</th><td>Halls of half-finished goods</td><td>Queues of unfinished cases and unanswered enquiries</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Motion</th><td>The operator reaching for a tool</td><td>Searching four systems to find one number</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Defects</th><td>Scrapped parts, rework</td><td>Bad data that has to be corrected further down the chain</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Ohno rated overproduction the worst, and the reasoning is worth noting: it creates all the others. Make more than is needed and you get inventory, transport, waiting and defects that are not discovered until long after they happened.</figcaption>
</figure>

Many have since added an eighth – **unused talent**: that the people who know
the job best are never asked. It was not on Ohno's list, and that is worth
saying plainly. But it points at something the system does assume, namely that
the improvements come from the people doing the work.

## The five principles

In *Lean Thinking* (1996) Womack and Jones set out five principles describing
the order the work should be done in. They have become the most common way into
lean outside the car industry.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">The order, as Womack and Jones set it out</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>1. Specify value</b><small>Seen from the customer, not from your own department. This step is the one most often skipped.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>2. Map the value stream</b><small>Every step from order to delivered. Including the ones nobody has drawn before.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>3. Create flow</b><small>Get the work moving without stalling in queues between the steps.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>4. Let the customer pull</b><small>Nothing is made until the next step actually asks for it.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>5. Pursue perfection</b><small>Not an end point, but never declaring it finished.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The order is not decoration. Create flow before you know what the customer values, and the result is that the wrong thing gets produced faster – a more expensive mistake than producing it slowly.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Pulling instead of pushing

This is the biggest practical break from conventional planning, and it is easy
to recognise for anyone who has worked with production planning.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Two ways to govern work</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Push</p>
      <h4>The plan decides</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Produced against a forecast and a plan</li>
        <li>Finished goods are pushed on to the next step</li>
        <li>When the forecast is wrong, inventory builds</li>
        <li>Classic MRP and ERP logic</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Pull</p>
      <h4>Demand decides</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Produced when the next step asks for it</li>
        <li>A signal triggers replenishment – that is what kanban is</li>
        <li>Inventory stays low because it is not refilled unprompted</li>
        <li>Requires short changeover times to be possible at all</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Ohno has described the idea as coming from American self-service shops: the shelf is refilled when someone has taken something, not according to a plan made in advance. The last bullet on the right matters – without short changeovers, small batches become too expensive, and the whole pull idea collapses.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is also where lean and a conventional ERP setup pull in opposite
directions. Material planning in ERP is push by design: the requirement is
calculated from forecasts and bills of material, and orders are released on
schedule. That does not rule out lean, but it does mean the two have to be set
up deliberately in relation to each other – otherwise you end up planning one
way and running the floor another.

## Muda, mura and muri

This is where Western lean parts company with Toyota, and it may be the most
useful part of the whole episode.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">The three Ms</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">The word</th><th scope="col">What it means</th><th scope="col">What it looks like</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Muda</th><td>Waste</td><td>The seven above. The only one most people look for.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Mura</th><td>Unevenness</td><td>A quiet Monday, chaos on Friday. Swings in load and staffing.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Muri</th><td>Overburden</td><td>People and machines run beyond capacity, over time.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The three hang together: unevenness creates overburden at the peaks and waste in the troughs. Hunt only for waste without doing anything about the swings and you get a system that looks efficient on paper and breaks in a busy week. Levelling – <i>heijunka</i> – is Toyota's answer to mura.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The toolbox

The Japanese words are one reason lean seems more mysterious than it is. Most
of them mean something fairly everyday.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">The words you will meet</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Word</th><th scope="col">Means</th><th scope="col">Used for</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Kaizen</th><td>Improvement</td><td>Many small improvements in daily work, made by the people doing it</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Kanban</th><td>Sign, signal</td><td>A card or an empty bin that says “refill” – the engine of pull</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Jidoka</th><td>Automation with a human touch</td><td>Equipment that stops itself on a fault, and the right to stop the line</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Poka-yoke</th><td>Guarding against slips</td><td>Design that makes fitting a part the wrong way physically impossible</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Heijunka</th><td>Levelling</td><td>Evening out volume and product mix, so the peaks disappear</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Gemba</th><td>The actual place</td><td>Going where the work happens and seeing for yourself, rather than reading a report</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">5S</th><td>Five words that all begin with s</td><td>Sort, set in order, shine, standardise and sustain</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Jidoka is the one most often left out when lean is installed, and the one that says most about the culture: an operator who sees something wrong has the right to halt the entire line. It is cheaper to stop for a minute than to make two hundred parts with the same fault.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Improvement as a habit

Kaizen is the part that separates lean from a project. The idea is not one big
push once a year, but small changes all the time, proposed and carried out by
the people doing the work.

The method used is a loop:

<figure class="fig fig--cycle">
  <p class="fig__title">PDCA – and then again</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__step">1</span><b>Plan</b><small>Describe the problem and what you think the cause is. State a measure that could turn out to be wrong.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">2</span><b>Do</b><small>Try it at small scale. Do not roll anything out across the organisation yet.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">3</span><b>Check</b><small>Measure it against something. Did it actually get better, or just different?</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">4</span><b>Act</b><small>If it worked, it becomes the new standard. If it did not, you start again with what you learned.</small></li>
  </ol>
  <p class="fig__loop">↻ And then it starts over on the next problem</p>
  <figcaption>The loop is usually credited to W. Edwards Deming, who himself pointed to Walter Shewhart and preferred the variant PDSA – study rather than check. The point is the same either way: a measure is a hypothesis until it has been measured.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Standardisation** belongs here too, and it is often misunderstood. A standard
in lean is not a rule meant to pin people down. It is the best known way *right
now*, and it is the starting point for the next improvement. Without a standard
you have nothing to measure the change against.

## The criticism

This is a walk through what lean is, and the objections belong in that. Several
of them are well founded.

**The word is used as cover for cutting staff.** The most common objection, and
it is not made up. “Lean” is often applied to cost-cutting exercises that have
nothing to do with the Toyota system, where the result is fewer people doing
the same work. That is precisely *muri*, and so the opposite of what the system
prescribes – but the label sticks anyway.

**Work pressure.** Even where lean is installed by the book, critics have
pointed out that a production system without slack can mean a higher and
steadier pace of work, and less room to breathe. The criticism has been raised
both in industry and in healthcare, and it deserves to be taken seriously
rather than dismissed as a misunderstanding.

**Supply chain fragility.** This is the objection that has gained the most
weight in recent years, and it deserves to stand clearly:

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Just-in-time is efficient and fragile for precisely the same reason: there is no inventory to fall back on.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>That is not a flaw in the method, but a cost of it.</b> Low inventories free up capital and expose problems quickly. Those same low inventories mean one stoppage anywhere propagates through the whole chain. The disruptions to world trade in recent years made that trade-off visible to everyone, and many organisations have since built buffers back in deliberately.</p>
</figure>

**Tools without culture.** The most common reason rollouts stall. 5S boards,
kanban cards and improvement boards are the visible part, and the easy part to
copy. The part that is hard – management actually going out into the operation,
an operator daring to stop the line, suggestions from the floor being carried
out – is the part that decides whether any of the rest matters. Install the
tools alone and they tend to be gone again within a year or two.

## What holds up

Once the decoration is stripped away, a few ideas are left that hold outside a
car factory too.

That it pays to look at **the flow through the whole chain** rather than
optimising each department separately – a department can become more efficient
and make the whole slower. That **queues of unfinished work** are a problem in
themselves, whether they are parts in a hall or cases in an inbox. That
**improvement should be a habit rather than a project**. And that the people
doing the work usually know best where it grates.

The rest – the words, the boards, the certifications – is packaging. Useful
packaging, but packaging.

*The content is a walk through what lean is and the criticism the method has
met, not a recommendation about how any particular organisation should be run.*
