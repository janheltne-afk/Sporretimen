**Robert Cialdini** is a social psychologist, and *Influence* (1984) is the
result of three years spent going undercover with car salesmen, telemarketers
and fundraisers to find out what they actually did. What he found was that they
used the same handful of tricks, over and over.

The premise is that people rely on **shortcuts**. We cannot weigh everything
carefully, so we use simple rules: do what the expert says, do what the others
do, give back when you receive. The rules are mostly sensible. The problem is
that they can be triggered deliberately.

## The six principles

- **Reciprocity.** When we receive something, we feel obliged to give back –
  including when we did not ask for it. The free sample and the pen from the
  charity appeal are not gifts. They are advances.
- **Commitment and consistency.** Once we have said or done something, we want
  to act in line with it. A small yes early makes a large yes later easier.
  Writing it down and saying it publicly strengthens the effect.
- **Social proof.** When uncertain, we look at what others are doing. “Best
  seller”, laugh tracks and full restaurants are all the same mechanism.
- **Liking.** We say yes more readily to people we like – and we like people who
  resemble us, who compliment us, and with whom we cooperate.
- **Authority.** Titles, uniforms and symbols of expertise trigger compliance,
  often without our checking whether the expertise is real.
- **Scarcity.** What is hard to get seems worth more. “Only three left” and
  “offer ends tonight” are loss aversion in practice.

In the revised 2021 edition Cialdini added a seventh: **unity** – that we say
yes to those we experience as part of *us*.

## How to defend yourself

The book is written as a defence manual, not a recipe, and each chapter ends
with how to notice the mechanism at work. The common thread: notice when you
feel a *pressure* to say yes that does not come from the matter itself. A gift
you did not ask for, a deadline that seems artificial, an authority that is not
relevant. That is the shortcut firing, not your judgement.

## What is disputed

The principles are among the most replicated findings in social psychology, and
have held up well through the replication crisis. But some of the individual
studies the book leans on have fared worse – particularly a few of the more
surprising effects tied to small, imperceptible cues. Read the broad strokes as
solid, and the most startling individual anecdotes with a caveat.

The other objection is ethical: the book today sells as much to those who want
to use the mechanisms as to those who want to resist them. Cialdini has answered
by distinguishing between *using* a principle that is genuinely present – the
item really is scarce – and *fabricating* it.
