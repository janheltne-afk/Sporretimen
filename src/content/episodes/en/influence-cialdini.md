**Robert Cialdini** is a social psychologist, and *Influence*, published in 1984, is
the result of three years spent going undercover with car salespeople,
telemarketers and fundraising organisations to find out what they actually did.
It is an unusual method for an academic, and it explains why the book has lasted:
it did not begin in the laboratory but on the floor, where people make a living
getting others to say yes.

What he found was that they used the same few moves, over and over – across
industries with nothing to do with one another.

## The shortcut, and why it exists

The starting point is not that people are stupid. It is that nobody has the
capacity to weigh everything carefully.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">How a shortcut is meant to work</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>A signal appears</b><small>A price, a title, a queue, a recommendation.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The rule fires</b><small>"Expensive means good." "The expert knows." "This many people can't be wrong."</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>You decide</b><small>Without going through the case point by point.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The point is that this mostly works. The rules are distilled experience, and in a life with hundreds of small choices a week they are necessary. The problem only arises when someone knows the rule better than you do, and places the signal deliberately.</figcaption>
</figure>

That asymmetry is what the whole book is about. Anyone who knows the trigger can
fire the response without supplying the thing the response was originally a
reaction to. A title without relevant competence. A scarcity that was manufactured.
A gift that is an invoice.

## Reciprocity

If we are given something, we feel obliged to give back. That holds even when we
did not ask for it, even when we do not like the giver, and even when what we got
is close to worthless.

What is interesting is not that the rule exists – it is probably a precondition for
human cooperation at all. What is interesting is that it is not very good at
measuring size.

<figure class="fig fig--scale">
  <p class="fig__title">What reciprocity does not do: weigh</p>
  <div class="fig__axis">
    <div class="fig__mark" style="left:10%"><b>What you got</b><small>A pen in the post, a free sample, a coffee</small></div>
    <div class="fig__mark" style="left:74%"><b>What you give back</b><small>A donation, a purchase, a favour that costs you something</small></div>
  </div>
  <p class="fig__ends"><span>Low value</span><span>High value</span></p>
  <figcaption>The rule says "give back", not "give back the same amount". And since it is the giver who decides what the first gift will be, it is also the giver who starts a debt whose size you never agreed to. The free sample and the pen from the fundraiser are not gifts. They are an advance.</figcaption>
</figure>

Reciprocity has a variant that is easier to fall for than the ordinary kind,
because it looks like the opposite of pressure. It works through concessions.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Two ways of asking for the same thing</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Straight out</p>
      <h4>Ask for what you want</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>You assess the request as it stands</li>
        <li>You say yes or no</li>
        <li>No debt is created</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">With a retreat</p>
      <h4>Ask for something large first</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>You say no to the large request</li>
        <li>The other person "gives ground" and asks for something smaller</li>
        <li>The concession feels like something you owe an answer to</li>
        <li>And the smaller request looks modest next to the first one</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Two things happen at once: you are given something to reciprocate, and you are given a yardstick that turns the real request into the reasonable option. The tell is that the first request was so large it can hardly have been meant seriously.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Commitment and consistency

Once we have said or done something, we want to act in line with it. A small yes
early makes a large yes later easier – not because the large request has improved,
but because a no now would make us inconsistent.

What makes this more than a curiosity is that the strength can be turned up.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Four things that make a commitment harder to walk away from</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>It was active</b><small>You said it, wrote it or did it – rather than merely thinking it.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>It was public</b><small>Other people know about it. Now your reputation is in it too.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>It cost something</b><small>Time, effort or discomfort. What you have struggled for, you defend harder.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>It felt freely chosen</b><small>The strongest of the four. If you were pressured, you can blame the pressure. If you chose, you have to own the choice.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Note the last one. It is why a mild request often sticks better than a threat: the threat gives you an explanation outside yourself, so you never have to change what you think about you. It is also why small, innocent yeses are worth noticing – it is not the first yes that costs, it is the third.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Social proof

When we are uncertain, we look at what others are doing. "Best seller", laugh
tracks, full restaurants and star ratings are all the same mechanism.

The rule is sensible: if many people have chosen something, that is often a sign it
holds up. But it is strongest exactly where it is least reliable.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">When other people's choices weigh most</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Strongest</p>
      <h4>Uncertainty and similarity</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The situation is unclear to you</li>
        <li>You have no experience of your own to measure against</li>
        <li>The others are like you</li>
        <li>There are many of them</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Weakest</p>
      <h4>When you know what you think</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>You have your own reference points</li>
        <li>You have tried the thing yourself</li>
        <li>The others are clearly unlike you</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The uncomfortable part is that the left-hand column describes precisely the situation in which you should trust the crowd least: you do not know enough to judge, and probably neither do they. Everyone is watching everyone, and nobody knows.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Liking

We say yes more readily to people we like. The trivial part is that this is true.
The useful part is knowing what "liking" is actually triggered by, because it is a
shorter list than you would think – and because everything on it can be
constructed.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">What liking actually hangs on</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Trigger</th><th scope="col">How it gets used</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Similarity</th><td>Shared home town, shared interest, matching dress, mirrored language</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Compliments</th><td>Work strikingly well, even when they are obviously overdone</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Familiarity</th><td>Repeated contact makes something familiar, and familiar feels safe</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Cooperation</th><td>"The two of us against the system" – the salesperson who goes in to fight your corner with the manager</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Association</th><td>The product stands next to something you already like, and some of it rubs off</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The defence here is not to stop liking people. It is to separate the two things at the moment you have to decide: have I started liking this person unusually quickly? And what does that have to do with the offer?</figcaption>
</figure>

## Authority

Titles, uniforms and other symbols of expertise trigger compliance, often without
our checking whether the expertise is real – or relevant.

The second is the more important qualification. Authority is a perfectly rational
shortcut when the expert really is an expert on the matter at hand. It fails in two
ways.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Two places authority slips</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The symbol without the substance</b><small>A title, a coat, an expensive car or a certificate on the wall can be worn by anyone. The symbols are easier to acquire than the competence they point to.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Expertise in the wrong field</b><small>A genuine authority speaks outside their area, and the authority travels with them – even though the knowledge does not.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Both have the same counter-question, and it is easy to ask politely: is this person an expert on precisely this, and what do they gain if I say yes? The second question is the more important one, and it is almost never rude to think it.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Scarcity

What is hard to get seems worth more. "Only three left" and "the offer ends
tonight" are loss aversion put to work: the prospect of losing something moves us
more than the prospect of gaining the equivalent.

Two variants are worth knowing because they bite harder than ordinary scarcity.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Two forms of scarcity that bite harder</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Newly scarce</p>
      <h4>It was available yesterday</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Something you had, or could have had, is disappearing</li>
        <li>Lands as a loss, not as a gain you missed</li>
        <li>Stronger than something that has always been rare</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Competed for</p>
      <h4>Other people want it too</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>"There is someone else viewing the flat"</li>
        <li>The bidding war, the queue, the visible rival</li>
        <li>Scarcity and social proof at once</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>What is worth noticing is what scarcity actually changes. It does not make the thing better. It makes you more aroused and worse at thinking – and that is precisely the state you are in when you are asked to decide.</figcaption>
</figure>

In the revised edition from 2021 Cialdini added a seventh principle: **unity** – we
say yes to those we experience as part of *us*, not merely as similar to us.
Kinship, place, shared history, "those of us who work here". The difference from
liking is that unity is not about liking someone, but about identity.

## The defence

The book is written as a defence manual, not a recipe, and each chapter ends with
how to notice the mechanism being used. The common thread comes down to one thing,
and it does not require you to see through anyone.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Notice whether the pressure to say yes comes from the case, or from something else.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>The signal is a feeling, not an analysis:</b> discomfort at saying no, a hurry you did not set yourself, warmth towards a stranger you have just met.<br /><b>The counter-question is always the same:</b> would I want this if I came across it online tomorrow, without the gift, the deadline, the queue or the person?<br /><b>And one practical move:</b> a debt you did not ask for does not have to be treated as a debt. You are allowed to say thank you for the pen and still say no.</p>
</figure>

It is also worth saying that a no does not require a justification. Much of the
force in these mechanisms lies in your feeling that you have to explain yourself,
and an explanation is something the other person can answer.

## What is contested

The principles are among the most repeated findings in social psychology, and have
held up well through the replication crisis. But individual studies the book leans
on have fared worse – particularly some of the more surprising effects tied to
small, imperceptible cues.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">How to read the book today</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Hold as solid</p>
      <h4>The broad strokes</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>That the principles exist</li>
        <li>That they work on most people</li>
        <li>That they are triggered by cues, not by judgement</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Hold with reservations</p>
      <h4>The individual examples</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The most striking anecdotes</li>
        <li>Precise effect sizes from single studies</li>
        <li>Effects of very small, imperceptible cues</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>This is a useful way to read far beyond this book: a framework can be right even if one of the examples illustrating it does not hold. And a single example can be fascinating without carrying the weight of a conclusion.</figcaption>
</figure>

The other objection is ethical, and it is real: the book today sells as much to
those who want to use the mechanisms as to those who want to defend themselves
against them. Cialdini has answered by distinguishing between *using* a principle
that is genuinely present – the item really is scarce, the expert really is an
expert – and *manufacturing* it.

Whether that distinction holds in practice is an open question. But it is at least
a distinction you can apply to yourself.
