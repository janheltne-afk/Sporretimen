**Rich Dad Poor Dad** has sold more than 40 million copies and is probably the
most widely read book on personal finance there is. It is also one of the most
criticised. Both facts deserve to be taken seriously, and that is what makes it
worth going through.

The book is built as a story about two father figures: the author's own father,
highly educated and securely employed, and "rich dad", a friend's father with
little formal education and his own business. The two give contradictory
advice, and the whole book is the reckoning between them.

## Assets and liabilities

The core is a definition. An **asset** is something that puts money in your
pocket. A **liability** is something that takes money out of it. No more
complicated than that.

It sounds trivial, but the consequence is uncomfortable: much of what we call
investments lands on the wrong side. A home you live in, with a mortgage,
insurance, maintenance and council charges, takes money out of your pocket
every month. A car does the same. By Kiyosaki's definition they are therefore
liabilities, whatever they are worth on paper.

Note that this is **not** the accounting definition. In a set of accounts the
house is an asset and the mortgage is a liability, separately. Kiyosaki uses
the words in his own way, and that is one of the things professionals object
to. His point is practical all the same: look at the cash flow, not at the
label.

## The rat race

The image he uses for the trap is a wheel. You get a higher salary, and spend
more. A bigger house, a more expensive car, higher fixed costs. Spending grows
in step with income, and you are as exposed as before – just at a higher level.

The way out is not through earning more, but through building income that does
not require you to show up at work. That is where he wants the surplus to go:
into something that returns cash flow.

## Working to learn

One of the better pieces of advice in the book has nothing to do with
investing. Choose a job by what you learn, not only by what it pays. Sales,
negotiation, accounting, communication – skills that follow you whatever you
end up doing.

## What the criticism is about

This has to be included, because the book is often sold without it:

- **"Rich dad" is probably not a real person.** Kiyosaki has never been able to
  document who he was, and has suggested in interviews that the figure is a
  construction.
- **The advice is vague.** The book tells you to acquire assets, but says
  little about how. The specifics sit in the courses and games sold alongside.
- **It recommends debt and leverage** without giving equal weight to what
  happens when the market turns. Several of the examples assume rising prices.
- **The definitions collide with professional terminology**, which makes the
  book hard to build on if you later read something more thorough.

## What still holds

The distinction between something that produces money and something that costs
money is a useful lens, particularly for anyone who has never thought about
money that way. So is the idea that financial literacy is a skill you can
train, and that a higher salary alone is not a plan.

Read it as a doorway that gets you thinking – not as a recipe to follow.
