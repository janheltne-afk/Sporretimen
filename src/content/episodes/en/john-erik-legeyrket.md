What is it really like to be a doctor? John Erik is a GP at Lundamo, grew up in
Alta and trained in Warsaw. The conversation turns out to be less about diseases
than you would expect, and more about communication, uncertainty, prioritising, and
what you do when there is no right answer.

## A choice that was never a single moment

He does not remember deciding. It was something he had settled on fairly early, and
from lower secondary school onwards he worked deliberately towards the grades he
needed.

It is worth noting what he does *not* say: he was not pushed. He experienced little
pressure at home about education and career. He did see it in others, though – some
of his fellow students had parents with clear expectations about medicine, even
where the student might have wanted something else. That theme comes back right at
the end of the conversation, and not by accident.

Outside work he describes himself as a son, a brother, a friend, a climber and a
traveller. The profession is an important part of his life, but not his whole
identity.

The degree itself is six years and 360 credits in Norway. After that, Norwegian
authorisation is required in order to practise, and anyone who wants to specialise
adds several more years on top.

## Why Poland

The answer is not the one you expect. It was not about points.

John Erik had been an exchange student in the United States, and wanted to study
abroad again. So he looked for medical degrees taught in English. Warsaw won out
because the city was large and international, and the university had an
English-language programme.

Moving from Alta – around 20,000 inhabitants – to a European capital was a big
change. He describes it as a positive one all the same, and the student environment
as international, with both Norwegian and foreign students.

## The degree, and the worst of it

He found his programme very theory-heavy, and has the impression that Norwegian
medical students get more independent patient contact and practical training
earlier. This is his personal experience, not a full comparison of every medical
degree in the two countries.

One practical problem was obvious: the patients spoke Polish. The solution was to
split students into groups where at least one person had the language. That person
could talk to the patient and help the rest of the group. It made placements
possible – but also more group-based and less independent.

He did think about quitting, but never seriously considered it. The first year was
particularly demanding: many subjects, frequent tests, long days and large volumes
of information.

And here is his answer to the question most people ask – do you have to be
exceptionally academic? Good academic foundations help, but he thinks the ability to
absorb *large volumes* of information matters at least as much. The degree demands
structure, stamina, independent work and the ability to keep going when the workload
is high.

## What it cost

These are numbers that are easy to underestimate. Tuition at his university was
around 110,000 Norwegian kroner a year. On top came loans for living costs.

## Five questions instead of one diagnosis

Something surprising follows from that: it is not always necessary to find the exact
diagnosis. With many common respiratory infections, for instance, the doctor does
not need to know precisely which virus it is, if the treatment and the advice end up
being the same either way.

What the doctor does have to answer is something else:

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">The assessment that comes before the name of the illness</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Could this be dangerous?</b><small>The first and most important filter.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Does it require treatment?</b><small>Not everything with a name should be treated.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Does it need rapid investigation?</b><small>Is it urgent, or can it wait?</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Is watchful waiting defensible?</b><small>Waiting is an active decision, not the absence of one.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>What should the patient watch for?</b><small>What should make the patient get back in touch?</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Notice the order, and you see that the diagnosis is not the goal in itself – it is a means of answering these five. The last point is also the one the patient should take out of the door with them.</figcaption>
</figure>

And then there is what he says about the content of the job, which surprised him: it
involves more handling of people than he had expected. Medical knowledge is the
foundation, but general practice is just as much about listening, explaining,
reassuring, setting boundaries, and helping people understand their own situation.

## The day

He starts around eight. His appointment book is set up with roughly 20 minutes per
consultation, with some slots left open to absorb delays.

It is worth being clear that this is *not* a standard: the time varies between
practices, doctors and the type of appointment booked. Simple problems take less
time. Mental health, long-running symptoms or complex presentations can take
considerably more.

A discharge summary, for the record, is the medical summary sent after a patient has
been treated at a hospital or an out-of-hours clinic: why the patient was examined or
admitted, what was found, what treatment was given, which medicines the patient
should take, what happens next, and what the GP has to follow up.

If the practice is covering daytime emergency duty, something quite different comes
on top: the doctor receives information from the emergency medical communication
centre about acute incidents locally, and assesses whether the patient should be
seen at the surgery, whether an ambulance should be sent, or whether the patient
needs to go straight to hospital. The details vary between municipalities and local
arrangements.

## When the doctor does not know

He answers no to the question of whether doctors always know what is wrong. Rashes,
symptoms and presentations he does not immediately recognise do occur.

What is interesting is what happens then – and that it is a system, not an
embarrassment.

On artificial intelligence the answer is nuanced. Use varies from doctor to doctor.
Some use approved documentation tools that can listen to the consultation and draft
a patient note. John Erik does not use one himself: he writes fast enough, and
thinks that checking and correcting an automatic note could potentially give him
*more* work.

And one boundary that is absolute: sensitive health information must not be entered
into ordinary, open language models. Any tool used has to be developed and approved
for processing patient data.

When he meets someone who has already decided, he tries to listen to the worry and
explain why he sees the situation differently. The patient's own information can be
valuable – it is the conclusion that is the problem, not the effort.

On angry patients the answer has two parts: he tries to stay calm and explain the
reasoning behind his assessment. Some need a thorough clinical explanation. Others
need clear boundaries. A doctor should not answer with anger, but neither should
they accept harassment or unacceptable behaviour at work.

On reviews on Legelisten, the Norwegian doctor review site, he tries not to think
about it too much. A negative review does not necessarily mean the treatment was
poor – a doctor sometimes has to say no to a prescription, an examination or a sick
note. Patient satisfaction and clinical quality are not always the same thing.

## Why more tests do not give a safer answer

This is the most counterintuitive part of the whole conversation, and it is worth
doing the arithmetic.

The reference range for a blood test is usually built to cover about 95 per cent of
a healthy reference group. That means around 5 per cent of healthy people get a
result outside the range – with nothing wrong with them. *(Source: legeforeningen.no)*

Take one test, and the chance of such a deviation is small. Take many, and the
picture changes quickly:

It is also why healthy people without symptoms or known risk factors usually do not
need a comprehensive health check every year. Targeted checks can still matter at
certain ages, or with particular illnesses, medicines, family history or risk
factors – and you should follow public screening programmes and recommended
vaccinations. *(Source: legeforeningen.no)*

A test result has to be assessed alongside symptoms, medical history and other
findings in any case.

## A few other myths he clears up

- **Antibiotic resistance affects the bacteria, not you.** It is the bacteria that become resistant to one or more antibiotics. Unnecessary and incorrect use helps resistant bacteria develop and spread. Antibiotics also do not work against viruses. *(Source: Helsenorge)*
- **Most adults need about seven to eight hours of sleep.** The need varies, but a stable sleep rhythm and good sleep quality matter too. Persistent sleep problems can affect mood, concentration, physical health and daily functioning. *(Source: Helsenorge)*
- **A glass of wine a day is not a health measure.** It is not possible to set a completely safe lower limit for alcohol intake. The risk depends on quantity, frequency, drinking pattern and state of health, and rises with consumption. *(Source: Helsenorge)*
- **Supplements should be used where there is a real need.** Vitamin D may be relevant for those who get little from diet and sunlight, and some groups are advised to take it. Iron supplements should normally be used where iron deficiency is established or likely – too much iron can also be harmful. A varied diet covers the need for many vitamins and minerals for most people. *(Source: Helsenorge)*
- **Travel vaccinations depend on the trip.** Destination, length of stay, activities, season, previous vaccinations and your own health all decide. Check the recommendations for the specific country well before departure. *(Source: Helsenorge)*
- **Doctors can write ordinary prescriptions for themselves – but should be careful.** It can be hard to assess your own health objectively. The Norwegian Directorate of Health advises that doctors should not prescribe addictive medicines to themselves or to close family, and a doctor cannot prescribe medicines on the reimbursed scheme for their own use. *(Source: the Norwegian Directorate of Health)*

On smartwatches he is personally sceptical about using them as a general health
tool. The measurements can confirm something the person already knows, or cause
unnecessary worry. They can motivate activity and pick up certain patterns, but a
measurement is not a diagnosis. Alerts that cause concern should be assessed
together with a health professional.

## How to get the most out of your appointment

This is the most directly usable part of the episode, and it costs you five minutes
in the waiting room.

No, you do not need to be highly extroverted. John Erik does not describe himself as
particularly outgoing in private, but steps into a professional role at work.
Communication can be learned and developed. Some specialties also involve less
continuous patient contact – radiology and pathology are the examples he gives.

Asked whether status and pay are motivation enough, he says no. The profession can
give security, respect and a good income, but the training is long and the
responsibility is heavy. Status and money alone will probably be weak motivation the
day the degree or the job becomes genuinely hard.

## Would he choose it again?

Yes. He thinks the training has given him an interesting profession, a secure job, a
good income and plenty of options. Had he not become a doctor, he might have
considered becoming a pilot.

And asked what he hopes patients remember him for, the answer is strikingly
undramatic: that he took them seriously. That he met them well, made thorough
assessments and gave the right treatment.

That may be the whole point. Medicine is about far more than making diagnoses and
writing prescriptions – it is about communicating, handling uncertainty,
prioritising time and meeting people in very different situations in life. And his
most important piece of advice is the same as the first section of this article:
choose it because *you* want it. Not because of pressure, status or other people's
expectations.
