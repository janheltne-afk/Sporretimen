Everything that crosses a border must have a number. Not a name – a number.

Goods to be cleared through customs must be classified: placed in one particular
code in the customs tariff. That code determines what you pay, which papers you
need, and whether the goods are allowed in at all.

This is one of those areas that looks like pure bureaucratic formality, and that
in practice is one of the most common and most expensive sources of error in
international trade.

## Six digits everyone agrees on

The foundation is the **HS nomenclature** – the Harmonized Commodity Description
and Coding System. The convention was signed in Brussels on 14 June 1983, and the
system is maintained by the **World Customs Organization**, the WCO. It is used by
the great majority of countries in the world.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">A Norwegian commodity code, digit by digit</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Digits 1–6: HS</b><small>International. These six are the same in Norway, the EU, China and the US. That is what lets a customs authority in one country understand a declaration from another.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Digit 7</b><small>National. Typically relates to the duty rate, which is bound in the WTO.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Digit 8</b><small>National. Covers statistical needs and the needs of, among others, the agriculture and foreign affairs authorities.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>That is why a code can look "almost the same" in two countries without being so. The first six match, while the tails part company. The EU uses eight digits in its Combined Nomenclature and ten in the TARIC database, while Norway stops at eight. If you are comparing a Norwegian code with a foreign one, the first six are the ones you can rely on.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Why it is harder than it sounds

Goods do not arrive labelled with their own code. Somebody has to decide which
shelf they belong on, and it is rarely obvious.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Questions that decide the code</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Question</th><th scope="col">Why it matters</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">What is the item made of?</th><td>Material often governs which chapter it lands in.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">What is it used for?</th><td>Function can override material, depending on the rules.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">How processed is it?</th><td>Raw material, semi-finished and finished goods are different codes.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Is it made up of several things?</th><td>Then it must be decided what gives the item its essential character.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Is it fully assembled?</th><td>Disassembled goods may be classified as the finished article, or as parts.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Is it a set?</th><td>Sets put up for retail sale follow their own rules.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The classic example is an item that can be two things at once. Is a hiking boot with a hard shell footwear or sports equipment? Is a smartwatch a watch or a communication device? The answer follows from the tariff's own interpretation rules and from the notes to the sections and chapters – not from what the item is called in marketing.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What the code actually decides

Many people think the commodity code is only about the duty rate. The duty rate is
often the least interesting part.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Four things the code determines</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Duty rate</b><small>What has to be paid, and whether a free trade agreement can give a lower rate.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Permits</b><small>Certain commodity codes trigger a requirement for a licence or approval before import.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Restrictions and prohibitions</b><small>Some codes are tied to sanctions, quotas or import bans.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Statistics</b><small>Trade statistics are built from the declarations. A wrong code gives a wrong picture of Norwegian imports and exports.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The second and third points are why a wrong code can be serious even when the duty rate is zero. You can import goods without underpaying a single krone and still have breached a licensing requirement you did not know existed – because the requirement hung on the code, not on the goods.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The responsibility sits with the importer

This is the point that surprises most people, and it is worth stating plainly.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">It is whoever imports the goods who is responsible for the classification being correct.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Not the customs authority:</b> Norwegian Customs checks, but does not establish the code for you in advance as a service.<br /><b>Not the supplier:</b> the code on an invoice from a foreign seller is a starting point, not an answer – it can be right in the country of dispatch and wrong in Norway.<br /><b>And not the forwarder alone:</b> the forwarder declares on your behalf, but you answer for the information.<br /><b>The consequence:</b> errors are often found afterwards, on inspection, and then retrospectively.</p>
</figure>

## The revisions

The HS nomenclature does not stand still. It is revised roughly every five years,
because goods that did not exist when the last version was written need somewhere
to live – and because goods that have become insignificant are merged.

The previous revision was worked into the Norwegian customs tariff from 1 January
2022. The next revision applies from 1 January 2028.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">A revision means goods move, even though the goods are the same.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>What happens:</b> codes are split, merged or withdrawn. An item that had one number can get another.<br /><b>Why it causes errors:</b> product catalogues, ERP systems and standing declaration templates contain codes that were correct yesterday.<br /><b>What it requires:</b> that somebody actually reviews the product range again at each revision. That is rarely anyone's explicit job, which is why it often does not get done.</p>
</figure>

## When you are unsure

There are few areas where "almost right" is as worthless. If you get stuck, there
are some established routes forward: the tariff's own interpretation rules and the
notes to the sections and chapters, Norwegian Customs' guidance on finding the
right commodity code, and – for goods to be imported regularly – the option of
asking for a binding classification ruling, so that you have a written answer to
point to on inspection.

That last one is underrated. It costs time once, and removes a risk that otherwise
runs along with every single shipment.

## What is worth taking with you

That the first six digits are international, and the only ones you can compare
across countries.

That the last two are Norwegian, and that the seventh relates to the duty rate and
the eighth to statistics.

That the code decides more than the duty rate – permits, restrictions and
statistics too.

That responsibility for correct classification sits with the importer.

And that the nomenclature is revised roughly every five years, with the next
version in the Norwegian tariff from 1 January 2028.

*This is a general account, not customs advice on a specific case. For binding
detail, the customs tariff with its interpretation rules and Norwegian Customs'
own decisions apply. Rates, codes and rules change, so always check current
information with Norwegian Customs.*
