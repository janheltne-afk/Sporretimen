**Mark Manson** was a blogger when, in 2016, he published the book with the
title nobody forgets. It sold in the millions, and was read by many who would
never have bought a book about values. That is what it is.

## You cannot care about everything

The core: you have a limited supply of care, time and attention, and most of
what we spend it on – what other people think, small things going wrong, whether
we are ahead of or behind the neighbours – does not deserve it. The point is not
indifference. It is choosing, deliberately, what is worth it.

Manson also inverts positive thinking. Constantly chasing feeling better is
itself a reminder that you do not feel good enough. He calls it the backwards
law: the harder you try to be happy, the less happy you become.

## Problems get swapped, not removed

A life without problems does not exist, and the solution to one problem is
usually a new one. That is not pessimism – it is a recommendation to choose your
problems with care. Happiness, Manson argues, comes from solving problems you
actually want to solve.

## Responsibility and fault

Perhaps the most useful distinction in the book. That something bad happened to
you may not be your fault. But how you relate to it now is your
responsibility – because there is nobody else who can take it. Fault is about the
past. Responsibility is about the next thing you do.

## Good and bad values

Manson proposes a test. Good values are reality-based, socially constructive and
within your own control: honesty, curiosity, standing for something. Bad values
are superstitious, destructive or dependent on others: popularity, always being
right, being liked by everyone. A lot of unhappiness, he argues, is measuring
yourself against values in the second group.

## The criticism

Most of this is old. Separating what you control from what you do not is
stoicism. That suffering comes from craving is Buddhism. That death gives life
meaning is Ernest Becker, whom Manson cites himself. The book partly admits
this, but sells it as new.

The tone is also a problem for many – profane, emphatic, and occasionally at
odds with itself. And the advice is general enough to fit everyone, which means
it does not quite fit anyone.

What holds up is the accessibility. For anyone who has never met these ideas,
this is a doorway that actually gets read.

*The content is a walk through what the book argues. Not mental health advice.*
