**Mark Manson** was a blogger when, in 2016, he published the book with the title
nobody forgets. It sold in the millions, and was read by many people who would never
have bought a book about values. That is what it is.

The title does a job here, and it is worth saying plainly: it promises indifference
and delivers the opposite. The book is not about ceasing to care. It is about the
fact that you care about too much, and that this is why you feel bad.

## You cannot care about everything

The core idea is a budget. You have a limited supply of care, time and attention, and
most of what we spend it on – what other people think, small things that go wrong,
whether we are ahead of or behind the neighbours – does not deserve it.

<figure class="fig fig--rings">
  <p class="fig__title">The budget, and where it actually goes</p>
  <div class="fig__rings">
    <div class="fig__ring"><b>Everything that could occupy you</b><small>News, other people's opinions, queues, lost arguments, comment sections, comparisons.</small></div>
    <div class="fig__ring"><b>What you actually spend it on</b><small>Usually whatever shouts loudest, not whatever matters most.</small></div>
    <div class="fig__ring" data-core><b>What deserves it</b><small>A few things. Manson thinks the list is shorter than most people assume.</small></div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The point is not indifference. It is that attention is zero-sum: caring less about something is not an attitude, it is a precondition for caring more about something else. Whoever does not choose has the choice made for them.</figcaption>
</figure>

Manson also inverts positive thinking, and this is the book's most original single
move.

<figure class="fig fig--cycle">
  <p class="fig__title">The backwards law</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__step">1</span><b>You want to feel better</b><small>A perfectly reasonable starting point.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">2</span><b>You pursue it</b><small>Reading, trying, striving.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">3</span><b>The pursuit reminds you of the lack</b><small>Striving for something presupposes that you do not have it.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">4</span><b>You feel worse</b><small>Not because of what is missing, but from measuring yourself against it.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">5</span><b>You try harder</b><small>And the circle tightens.</small></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The harder you try to be happy, the less happy you become. That is not an argument for giving up – it is an argument for moving your attention from the state you want to the thing you actually do.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Problems are exchanged, not removed

There is no such thing as a life without problems, and the solution to one problem is
usually a new one. That is not pessimism – it is a recommendation to choose your
problems carefully.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">The question that actually moves something</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The usual question</b><small>"What do I want?" Everybody wants the fitness, the career, the relationship. The answer distinguishes nobody from anybody.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Manson's question</b><small>"What pain am I willing to sustain?" Who is willing to train when it is boring, work when nobody is watching, have the difficult conversations?</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>That answer is what decides</b><small>You do not get what you wish for. You get what you are willing to struggle for.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Happiness, Manson argues, comes from solving problems you actually want to solve. That makes the choice of problems more important than the choice of goals – and it is a far more usable test when you are standing between two paths.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Responsibility and fault

Perhaps the most useful distinction in the book, and the easiest one to get wrong.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Two words that are constantly confused</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Fault</p>
      <h4>Is about the past</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Who caused it</li>
        <li>Can lie with someone else</li>
        <li>Can be entirely outside your control</li>
        <li>Changes nothing going forward</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Responsibility</p>
      <h4>Is about what you do next</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>How you relate to it now</li>
        <li>Always lies with you</li>
        <li>Not because it is fair, but because nobody else can take it</li>
        <li>Is the only thing that can change anything</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>That something painful happened to you may well not be your fault. But how you relate to it now is your responsibility. The distinction is worth holding on to precisely because it is unfair: it does not ask you to absolve anyone, it only asks you to stop waiting for them to put it right.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Good and bad values

Manson proposes a test, and it is simple enough to actually use.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Manson's test for a value</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Good values</p>
      <h4>You control them yourself</h4>
      <ul><li>Honesty</li><li>Curiosity</li><li>Standing for something</li><li>Reality-based</li><li>Socially constructive</li></ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Bad values</p>
      <h4>They depend on other people</h4>
      <ul><li>Popularity</li><li>Always being right</li><li>Being liked by everyone</li><li>Superstitious or destructive</li><li>Dependent on something outside you</li></ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Much unhappiness, Manson argues, is measuring yourself against something in the right-hand column – where you do not have your hands on the wheel anyway. The test is not whether the value sounds admirable, but whether you can succeed at it without somebody else having to do something specific.</figcaption>
</figure>

There is also a reason "always being right" sits on the wrong side, and it is worth
drawing out: the value makes it impossible to learn anything. Manson argues that
uncertainty is a precondition for development – if you are certain, there is nothing
left to find out.

## Death as the yardstick

The book's final chapter is also its most serious, and the tone shifts completely.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Most of what you worry about means nothing seen from the end.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Why Manson includes it:</b> he uses death as the ultimate test of what deserves your attention – a filter that is brutal, but effective.<br /><b>Where it comes from:</b> he credits Ernest Becker, who argued that much human behaviour is an attempt to leave behind something that outlasts the body.<br /><b>What it does to the rest of the book:</b> without this chapter it is a collection of advice. With it, it becomes an argument about what a life is for.</p>
</figure>

## The criticism

Most of this is old, and it is worth knowing where it comes from.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Where the ideas actually originate</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">The idea in the book</th><th scope="col">Where it comes from</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Separate what you control from what you do not</th><td>Stoicism</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Suffering comes from desire</th><td>Buddhism</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Death gives life meaning</th><td>Ernest Becker, whom Manson cites himself</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The book partly admits this, but sells it as new. That is not in itself an objection to the content – old ideas can be right – but it is worth knowing that originals exist, and that they are more precise.</figcaption>
</figure>

The tone is also a problem for many – sweary, emphatic, and at times at odds with
itself. A book insisting that you are not special spends a great deal of space on the
author's own stories. A book warning against the promises of the self-help genre is
itself a self-help book with a promise in the title.

And the advice is general enough to fit everyone, which means it does not quite fit
anyone. "Choose better values" is hard to disagree with and hard to do anything about
tomorrow morning.
