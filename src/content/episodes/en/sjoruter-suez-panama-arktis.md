Sea transport is the cheapest way to move something heavy a long way. That is why
it pays to produce in Asia and sell in Europe, and why it has paid for decades.

But cheap over distance does not mean robust. Almost all of world trade is
squeezed through a small number of narrow places, and that is where the
vulnerability sits.

This episode is about the most important of them, what actually constrains each
one, and what happens when one of them closes.

## Why a handful of places decide so much

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">A ship can go almost anywhere. It is the land in between that forces it through particular points.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Without Suez</b> goods between Asia and Europe have to go around the whole of Africa.<br /><b>Without Panama</b> goods between Asia and the US east coast have to go around South America, or be unloaded and carried overland.<br /><b>The consequence:</b> two canals built more than a century ago still decide what things cost in a shop in Norway.</p>
</figure>

## Suez

The Suez Canal opened in 1869 and links the Mediterranean to the Red Sea. Its most
important technical feature is something it does *not* have: locks.

The canal runs at sea level the whole way, between two seas that sit at roughly
the same height. That means no lifting, no water consumption and no capacity limit
tied to rainfall. The constraint in Suez is width, depth and traffic management –
how many ships can pass each other, and how deep they sit.

It is also why a grounding can paralyse the canal completely. Without locks there
are no parallel chambers to route traffic through.

## Panama

The Panama Canal opened in 1914 and links the Atlantic to the Pacific. It is
shorter than Suez, but technically far more complicated, because it does not run
at sea level. Ships are lifted up to a lake, cross it, and are lowered again.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">Why Panama is vulnerable in a completely different way</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The ship is lifted in locks</b><small>Up to Gatún Lake, which sits well above sea level.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Every lock operation uses fresh water</b><small>The water runs out to sea when the ship is lowered again.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The water comes from rainfall</b><small>The lake is filled by rain in the surrounding watershed.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Drought becomes a capacity limit</b><small>Less rain means fewer transits per day, and a lower permitted draft.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>This is the most counterintuitive thing in the episode. Panama's constraint is not space, and not demand. It is <em>water</em>. The same canal can take far fewer ships in a dry year than in a wet one, with nothing physically changed – and the canal competes for the same water as the country's drinking supply.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Panamax and Neopanamax

Because the locks have fixed dimensions, the Panama Canal has given its name to a
whole class of ship. A vessel is not built larger than the canal it has to pass.

## The Arctic routes

It is worth separating two things that often get conflated here: the Northern Sea
Route north of Russia, and the Northwest Passage through the Canadian archipelago.

The argument for both is the same, and it is real: the distance between north-east
Asia and northern Europe is considerably shorter over the top than through Suez.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Why shorter is not the same as better</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Obstacle</th><th scope="col">What it means in practice</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Ice</th><td>The route is not open year-round, and ice conditions vary from year to year.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Ship class</th><td>Ordinary container ships are not ice-strengthened. It takes purpose-built, more expensive vessels.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Escort</th><td>Icebreaker escort costs money, and has to be planned.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Insurance</th><td>Higher risk means higher premiums, and some underwriters will not cover it.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Emergency response</th><td>Long distances to port, little infrastructure, and an environment where a spill is extremely hard to clean up.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Predictability</th><td>Container lines sell fixed sailings. A route that varies with the ice suits liner traffic badly.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The last point is the most commonly underestimated. Liner shipping is not about being fastest once, but about being able to promise the same arrival day every week, all year. A route that is three weeks faster in summer and closed in winter does not solve that problem – it creates it.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The detour

When a main route cannot be used, alternatives exist. They are simply expensive in
a way that is not obvious.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">What happens when ships have to go around Africa</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The voyage gets longer</b><small>Several extra weeks on Asia–Europe, and more fuel per trip.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Ships are occupied for longer</b><small>A ship on a detour is out of the market for that extra time. The fleet shrinks without a single vessel disappearing.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Capacity tightens</b><small>Fewer available ships against the same demand raises freight rates – including on routes that are not affected.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Schedules slip</b><small>Port calls shift, containers end up in the wrong place, and empties pile up where they are not needed.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Step two is the key, and the one that explains why a disruption in one place raises prices everywhere. The effect is not that the route becomes more expensive. The effect is that total transport capacity in the world becomes smaller, because every ship spends longer on every trip.</figcaption>
</figure>

Since late 2023, much of the traffic between Asia and Europe has gone around the
Cape of Good Hope instead of through the Red Sea and Suez, because of the security
situation. The scale has fluctuated considerably, and several carriers have
switched back and forth as the risk picture has changed. Traffic volume figures
should therefore always be taken from up-to-date sources, not from an article.

## How to read the news about this

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Always ask what the binding resource is.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>In Suez:</b> width, depth and traffic management.<br /><b>In Panama:</b> fresh water.<br /><b>In the Arctic:</b> ice, season and predictability.<br /><b>And in the market as a whole:</b> how many ship-weeks are available – not how many ships exist.</p>
</figure>

## What is worth taking with you

That sea transport is cheap over distance, but vulnerable at a few narrow points.

That Suez has no locks, which both makes it simple and makes it easy to block
completely.

That Panama's constraint is water, not space.

That Arctic routes are shorter, but lose on predictability – which is what liner
shipping actually sells.

And that a detour does not only cost the ships taking it, because it pulls
capacity out of the whole market.

daily transit numbers, the Panama and Suez canal authorities' own notices apply.*
