After *Surrounded by Idiots*, **Thomas Erikson** wrote a series of books about
difficult people. *Surrounded by Psychopaths* (2017) is about manipulation: how
to recognise it, and what to do.

We use the episode to go through the subject more thoroughly than the book
does – because here the professional grounding is actually good, but it is
applied very loosely in everyday speech.

## What psychopathy is

Psychopathy is not a word for people who are unkind, cold or difficult. It is a
clinical concept, and the most widely used assessment tool is the **PCL-R**,
developed by the psychologist **Robert Hare**. It assesses, among other things,
superficial charm, grandiosity, pathological lying, lack of empathy and remorse,
impulsivity, and failure to accept responsibility.

Two things are worth noting. First, it is an **assessment**, not a test you take
yourself: it requires clinical training, an interview and a review of records.
Second, psychopathy is not a separate diagnosis in DSM-5 – what exists there is
*antisocial personality disorder*, which overlaps but is not the same thing.

## How common it is

Estimates vary, and that is part of the point. Around one per cent of the
population is a figure often cited, with higher rates in prison populations and
somewhat elevated rates in certain occupations. Claims that a large percentage
of executives are psychopaths circulate widely and rest on thin material.

By comparison, a great many people have **individual traits** – low empathy for
a period, high self-regard, an episode of lying. Having traits is not having the
condition.

## The pattern the book describes

What the book does best is describe the sequence as people actually experience
it:

- **Charm first.** It is almost always positive at the start. That is not a
  counter-argument – it is part of the pattern, and the reason people around you
  do not believe you later.
- **Gradual boundary testing.** Small breaches, each too small to raise.
- **Inverting reality.** You are misremembering, you are too sensitive, you
  started it.
- **Splitting.** Different stories to different people, so nobody has the whole
  picture.
- **Responsibility always lies elsewhere.** Never with the person concerned.

## Why the word is used too loosely

Because it offers an explanation, and because it offers an enemy. The problem is
that the label does not help you: it turns the conflict into a question of what
someone *is* rather than what they *do*, and it is nearly impossible to move on
from.

It is also unfair. The vast majority of difficult people are not psychopaths.
They are stressed, badly managed, badly trained, selfish, or simply in
disagreement with you.

## What actually helps

- **Describe behaviour, not the person.** “He has changed the agreement three
  times without saying so” is usable. “He is a psychopath” is not.
- **Write it down.** Documentation is the only thing that holds when reality is
  disputed.
- **Get other people's eyes on it.** The pattern is easier to see from outside.
- **Reduce contact where you can.** Distance beats argument.
- **Seek help where it is serious.** A safety representative, union
  representative, doctor or lawyer – depending on what it concerns.

*The content is general information about a clinical concept, not a diagnostic
tool. If you suspect you are in a harmful relationship, contact your doctor or
other qualified help.*
