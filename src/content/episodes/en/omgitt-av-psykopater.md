After *Surrounded by Idiots*, **Thomas Erikson** wrote a series of books about
difficult people. *Surrounded by Psychopaths*, published in 2017, is about
manipulation: how to recognise it, and what to do.

We use the episode to go through the subject more thoroughly than the book does –
because here the professional foundation is actually good, but the word is used very
loosely in everyday speech. That makes this an unusual topic: the problem is not that
the knowledge is missing, but that the word has got away from it.

## What psychopathy is

Psychopathy is not a word for people who are nasty, cold or difficult. It is a
clinical term, and the most widely used assessment instrument is the **PCL-R**,
developed by the psychologist **Robert Hare**.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Some of what the PCL-R looks for</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Area</th><th scope="col">Examples of what is assessed</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Interpersonal</th><td>Superficial charm, grandiosity, pathological lying</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Affective</th><td>Lack of empathy, lack of remorse, failure to accept responsibility</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Lifestyle</th><td>Impulsivity, need for stimulation, lack of realistic long-term goals</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Note that none of the items is about being unpleasant, domineering or hard to work with. That runs through the whole list: it describes something other than what most people mean when they use the word.</figcaption>
</figure>

Two things are worth noting in particular.

  <p class="fig__title">What this is not</p>

    <div class="fig__col">

        <li>Is based on interview</li>

      </ul>

  </div>
  <figcaption>Both qualifications point the same way: this is not something you can establish about a colleague, an ex or a boss, however well you recognise the pattern. And it is not something you need to establish in order to handle your own situation.</figcaption>
</figure>

## How common it is

Estimates vary, and that is part of the point. Around one per cent of the population
is a figure often used, with higher rates in prison populations and somewhat elevated
rates in certain occupational groups. Claims that many per cent of managers are
psychopaths circulate widely and rest on thin material.

<figure class="fig fig--scale">
  <p class="fig__title">Traits and condition are not the same</p>
  <div class="fig__axis">
    <div class="fig__mark" style="left:12%"><b>Individual traits</b><small>Very many people. Low empathy for a period, high self-confidence, an episode of lying.</small></div>
    <div class="fig__mark" style="left:88%"><b>Clinical assessment</b><small>Around one per cent. Requires training, interview and a review of documentation.</small></div>
  </div>
  <p class="fig__ends"><span>Common</span><span>Rare</span></p>
  <figcaption>A great many people have individual traits from the list, in some periods. Having traits is not having the condition, and the distance between the two is the whole point.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The pattern the book describes

What the book does best is describe the sequence as people actually experience it. It
is worth setting out in order, because the order itself explains why it is so hard to
get out of.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">The sequence, as it is experienced</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The charm first</b><small>It is almost always positive at the start – often strikingly so.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Gradual boundary testing</b><small>Small overstepping, each too small to raise without seeming unreasonable.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Reality is inverted</b><small>You are misremembering, you are too sensitive, you started it.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Splitting</b><small>Different stories to different people, so that nobody has the whole picture.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Responsibility always lies elsewhere</b><small>Never with the person concerned.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Step two is what makes the rest possible. Each individual incident is calibrated to be too small to raise – and when you finally raise the sum of them, it is the sum that sounds exaggerated.</figcaption>
</figure>

The charm deserves a note of its own, because it is often misread as a
counter-argument.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">That it was good at the start is not a sign that you are wrong. It is part of the pattern.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Why it makes things worse:</b> everyone else still has version one. They have no reason to believe your version two.<br /><b>Why it makes you doubt yourself:</b> you remember how it was too, and it is easier to think you broke something than that it was never what you thought.<br /><b>The practical point:</b> a relationship is judged on how it is now, not on how it opened.</p>
</figure>

Splitting is the part that leaves you standing alone, and it is worth seeing clearly.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Why nobody sees what you see</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">What the others have</p>
      <h4>One piece each</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>One version of the incident</li>
        <li>A pleasant personal experience</li>
        <li>No reason to compare</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">What you have</p>
      <h4>The whole, without witnesses</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The pattern over time</li>
        <li>Contradictory statements you cannot document</li>
        <li>A story that grows less believable the more complete it is</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>This is why the single most useful piece of advice is to talk to someone. Not to be agreed with, but because splitting only works for as long as people do not compare notes.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Why the word is used too readily

Because it supplies an explanation, and because it supplies an enemy. The problem is
that the label does not help you: it turns the conflict into a question of what
somebody *is* rather than what they *do*, and it is nearly impossible to move on
from.

It is also unfair. The vast majority of difficult people are not psychopaths.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Far more likely explanations</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Explanation</th><th scope="col">How it looks from outside</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Stress</th><td>Short fuse, forgotten commitments, poor communication for a period</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Badly managed</th><td>Contradictory instructions passed downwards without being sorted out</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Badly trained</th><td>Nobody has taught the person how to give uncomfortable feedback</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Self-interest</th><td>Ordinary selfishness, which is widespread and not clinical</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Disagreement</th><td>The person sincerely thinks something different from you</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>None of these makes the behaviour acceptable, and you do not have to put up with any of it. But they can be handled, and a condition cannot. That is the practical reason to try them first.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What actually helps

The advice below all comes down to the same move: shift the focus from who the person
is to what is actually happening – and make what is happening checkable.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Five moves, in the order that gives most</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Describe behaviour, not the person</b><small>"He has changed the agreement three times without saying so" is usable. "He is a psychopath" is not – it can neither be confirmed nor acted on.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Write it down</b><small>Documentation is the only thing that holds when reality is disputed. Dates, verbatim quotes, short and matter-of-fact.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Get someone else's view</b><small>The pattern is easier to see from outside, and it breaks the splitting.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Reduce contact where you can</b><small>Distance beats argument. You do not win a discussion with someone who is not conducting it in good faith.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Seek help where it is serious</b><small>Safety representative, union representative, GP, lawyer – depending on what it concerns.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Note that none of the five requires you to be right about the cause. They work equally well whether the person is manipulative, stressed or simply in disagreement with you – and that is precisely why they are safe to use.</figcaption>
</figure>

## About the book

Erikson is readable, and he lands the recognition. But the title does something
unfortunate to a term that is already struggling: it implies that psychopaths are
something one is surrounded by, which is in practice the opposite of what the figures
say.

The series has also drawn substantial criticism for the scientific basis of the
personality model it builds on, which is covered in more detail in the episode on
*Surrounded by Idiots*. For this subject in particular it is worth saying that the
field behind the concept of psychopathy is solid – it is the use of the word outside
that field that is not.

*This is general information about a clinical term, not a diagnostic tool. If you
suspect you are in a harmful relationship, contact your GP or other qualified help.*
