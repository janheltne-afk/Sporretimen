**Malcolm Gladwell** writes for *The New Yorker* and has an unusual gift for
turning research into narrative. *Outliers* (2008) asks what separates those who
succeed extremely from everyone else – and answers that it is far less talent
and far more circumstance than we like to think.

## Birth month

The book opens with a table of Canadian hockey players, in which a striking
majority are born in January, February and March. The reason: the age cut-off
falls on 1 January, so those born early in the year are nearly a full year older
than the youngest in the same cohort. They are bigger, get selected, get better
coaches and more ice time – and the advantage compounds.

That is the **Matthew effect**: to those who have, more will be given. Small
accidental early advantages become large advantages later, because they buy more
practice.

## 10,000 hours

The book's most famous idea. Gladwell takes the psychologist K. Anders
Ericsson's studies of violinists, in which the best had practised around 10,000
hours by their early twenties, and uses the Beatles' nights in Hamburg and Bill
Gates's access to a computer as a teenager as illustrations.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">10,000 hours is not a threshold. It was an average.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Ericsson himself, in <i>Peak</i> (2016):</b> the number varies enormously between fields, and what matters is <i>deliberate</i> practice with feedback – not hours logged.</p>
</figure>

This has to be said plainly: **Ericsson believed Gladwell had misread him.** In
his own book *Peak* he writes that 10,000 was an average, not a threshold; that
what matters is *deliberate* practice with feedback, not hours logged; and that
the number varies enormously between fields. The rule has become a cliché the
book does not actually support.

## Opportunity at the right time

Gates, Steve Jobs and several founders of the computer industry were born within
a few years of 1955 – old enough to seize the personal computer when it arrived,
young enough not to be settled. Gladwell's point is not that they were not able.
It is that able people are everywhere, and it is the opportunity that is rare.

## Cultural legacy

The most contested section. Gladwell explains plane crashes through
communication culture in the cockpit, and East Asian mathematics results through
the work ethic of rice farming. The idea that culture shapes how we work is
solid. The individual examples have been criticised as oversimplified and partly
wrong.

## Intelligence has a threshold

One of the book's more robust points: above a certain level, higher IQ does not
produce more success. The Terman study followed highly gifted children through
life, and what distinguished those who succeeded was not intelligence but
upbringing and the ability to handle people.

## The criticism

The book argues from anecdote, and picks the anecdotes that fit. It never shows
how many people got the same opportunity as Gates and did not become Gates. And
its most cited rule does not hold as presented.

What holds up is the central idea: that success is less individual than we
think, and that “what opportunities did they get?” is a more revealing question
than “how able were they?”.
