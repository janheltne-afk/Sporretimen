The fourth book in the series takes on narcissism. As with psychopathy, the
starting point is a genuine professional concept that has become an everyday
word – and there is more to gain from clearing up the terms than from hunting
for narcissists.

## Traits and diagnosis

**Narcissistic traits** are held by more or less everyone, to varying degrees
and in varying periods: wanting to be seen, overrating one's own contribution,
handling criticism poorly. It is a normal part of being human, and it rises and
falls through life.

**Narcissistic personality disorder** is something else. It is a diagnosis with
criteria in DSM-5 – persistent grandiosity, need for admiration, lack of
empathy – which must cause significant difficulty over time and across several
areas of life. Estimates sit around one to two per cent of the population, and
the diagnosis is made by health professionals after assessment.

The distance between the two is the whole point. A colleague who talks a lot
about themselves has traits. That is not the same as a condition.

## Two forms

This is the most useful nuance, and the one most often missing from popular
accounts.

- **Grandiose narcissism.** What people picture: self-assured, dominant,
  demands attention, responds to criticism with aggression.
- **Vulnerable narcissism.** The same core – a fragile self-image and a strong
  need for validation – but outwardly insecure, sensitive, withdrawn and easily
  offended. This form does not look self-assured at all, and is therefore rarely
  spotted.

The common denominator is not high self-esteem. It is self-esteem that tolerates
very little, and has to be propped up from outside.

## The patterns people recognise

What the book does well is describing how it feels to be close to someone with
strong traits: that conversations turn back, that criticism meets either
counter-attack or collapse, that it alternates between idealisation and
devaluation, and that you gradually start adjusting yourself to avoid the
reaction.

That is a recognisable and real experience, and it is worth describing without
anyone having to be diagnosed for it to be valid.

## Why the word is used too loosely

Because it explains pain, and because it assigns blame. But the label rarely
makes anything better. It makes the conflict permanent – you cannot negotiate
with a condition – and it is used almost exclusively about people we disagree
with or find painful to be near.

The claim that social media has created an epidemic of narcissism circulates
widely. Research on generational differences in narcissistic traits is contested,
and the findings diverge. It is safer to say that the platforms reward
self-presentation than that they have changed personalities.

## What helps

On the behaviour: describe it concretely, hold your own boundaries, accept that
you cannot negotiate your way to empathy, and get support from outside. On the
diagnosis: that belongs with health professionals, and treatment exists – but it
requires the person to want it, and that is precisely what is often missing.

*The content is general information about a clinical concept, not a diagnostic
tool. If you recognise yourself in a difficult relationship, contact your doctor
or other qualified help.*
