The fourth book in the series takes on narcissism. As with psychopathy, the starting
point is a genuine clinical term that has become an everyday word – and there is more
to be gained from sorting out the terms than from hunting for narcissists.

So we spend most of the space on two things: what the word actually means, and what
helps when you are close to someone who is hard to be close to. Neither requires you
to make a diagnosis, and that is deliberate.

## Traits and diagnosis are not the same thing

**Narcissistic traits** are present in almost everyone, to varying degrees and in
varying periods: wanting to be seen, overestimating your own contribution, taking
criticism badly. It is a normal part of being human, and it rises and falls through
life.

**Narcissistic personality disorder** is something else. It is a diagnosis with
criteria in DSM-5 – persistent grandiosity, a need for admiration, a lack of empathy
– which must cause significant difficulty over time and across several areas of life.
Estimates put it at around one to two per cent of the population, and it is diagnosed
by health professionals after assessment.

## The core

One thing ties it all together, and it is easier to remember than any list of
criteria.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">It is not high self-esteem. It is self-esteem that can take very little, and that has to be propped up from outside.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>That is why admiration is not a bonus but a necessity:</b> it fills a hole that is not filled from within.<br /><b>That is why criticism lands so disproportionately hard:</b> it is not an objection to an action, but to the whole foundation.<br /><b>And that is why two completely different people appear to have the same problem:</b> the one who inflates and the one who withdraws are defending the same fragile self-image with different strategies.</p>
</figure>

## The pattern people recognise

What the book does well is describe what it is like to be close to someone with
strong traits. One pattern in particular recurs, and it is worth drawing, because it
is easier to recognise as a circle than as separate incidents.

<figure class="fig fig--cycle">
  <p class="fig__title">The swing between up and down</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__step">1</span><b>Idealisation</b><small>You are exceptional, you understand things nobody else does, you are the only one who counts.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">2</span><b>Expectation</b><small>You are to deliver what that picture requires – attention, loyalty, confirmation.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">3</span><b>Disappointment</b><small>You do something ordinarily human: disagree, are busy, say no.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">4</span><b>Devaluation</b><small>The picture flips. You were never who you were told you were.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">5</span><b>Restoration</b><small>The warmth comes back, and you are relieved – which makes the next round easier to step into.</small></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>What makes the circle demanding is point five. If it were only devaluation, most people would leave. It is the swing that binds, because the relief that things are good again feels stronger than a steady, good relationship ever does.</figcaption>
</figure>

Alongside the swing, the book describes something less dramatic but often more
corrosive: the slow adjustment of yourself.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">How you gradually shrink yourself</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>You notice what triggers a reaction</b><small>A subject, a tone, a type of question.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>You steer around it</b><small>Not out of fear, at first – just because it is easier.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>The list grows</b><small>More subjects, more phrasings, more people you would rather not mention.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>You start doubting your own experience</b><small>Was it really that bad? Am I exaggerating?</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>You stop raising it with others</b><small>And then there is nobody left to reflect it back to you.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>This is a recognisable and real experience, and it is worth describing without anyone having to be diagnosed for it to be valid. The final step is the most important one to break: telling one person outside what is actually happening does more than finding the right word for the other person.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Why the word is used too readily

Because it explains pain, and because it assigns blame. That is an understandable
impulse. But the label rarely makes anything better.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Label or description?</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">"They are a narcissist"</p>
      <h4>What it does</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Makes the conflict permanent – a condition cannot be negotiated with</li>
        <li>Moves the conversation to what the person is</li>
        <li>Is impossible to answer without appearing to confirm it</li>
        <li>Is used almost exclusively about people we disagree with</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">"When I say no, I get three days of silence"</p>
      <h4>What it does</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Describes something that actually happened</li>
        <li>Can be confirmed or disputed</li>
        <li>Lets you set a boundary without making a diagnosis</li>
        <li>Holds just as well if you are wrong about the cause</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The practical advantage of the right-hand column is that it does not require you to be right about something you cannot know. You do not need to know why a person does something in order to decide that you will not live with it.</figcaption>
</figure>

## On the claim of an epidemic

The claim that social media has created an epidemic of narcissism circulates widely,
and it deserves careful handling.

Research on generational differences in narcissistic traits is contested, and the
findings diverge. It is safer to say that the platforms reward self-presentation than
that they have changed personalities. Those are two entirely different claims: the
first is about what it pays to do, the second about who people have become.

## What helps

Here it is useful to separate two tracks sharply, because they belong with completely
different people.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Two tracks that should not be mixed</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">What you can do</p>
      <h4>About the behaviour</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Describe it concretely, not as personality</li>
        <li>Hold your own boundaries, and expect them to be tested</li>
        <li>Accept that you cannot negotiate your way to empathy</li>
        <li>Get support from outside, so you have something to measure the experience against</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">What you should not do</p>
      <h4>About the diagnosis</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>It belongs with health professionals, after assessment</li>
        <li>Treatment exists</li>
        <li>But it depends on the person wanting it</li>
        <li>And that is precisely what is often missing</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The third point on the left is the hardest to take in, and at the same time the most freeing: you cannot argue someone into understanding how you feel. You can only decide what you do about it.</figcaption>

## About the book

Erikson is good on recognition and weak on precision. The strength is that he puts
words to an experience many people have, without requiring the reader to be a
professional. The weakness is the same as in the rest of the series: the explanations
lean on typologies, and the line between trait and condition sometimes blurs in a
genre that sells best when it is sharp.

For this subject that blurring is more than a cosmetic flaw, because a diagnostic
term that comes loose from its clinical meaning is hard to put back. Read the book
for the descriptions, not for the categories.

*This is general information about a clinical term, not a diagnostic tool. If you
recognise yourself in a difficult relationship, contact your GP or other qualified
help.*
