In 1926 the American publisher **George S. Clason** issued a series of short
pamphlets on saving, written as parables from ancient Babylon. Banks and
insurance companies handed them out, and they have since been gathered into one
of the best-selling books on personal finance there is.

The form is old-fashioned – stories about Arkad, the richest man in Babylon,
explaining how he got there. But the advice is strikingly familiar, and some of
it is exactly what modern books repeat.

## Pay yourself first

The best-known piece of advice is also the simplest: **set aside a tenth of
everything you earn, before you pay anything else.**

The order is the whole point. If you save what is left when the month is over,
there is rarely anything. If you save first, spending adapts to the rest. It is
the same mechanism that is now called automatic saving, built on the fact that
willpower is a poor plan.

## The seven cures

The book sums itself up in seven points, given here in modern language:

1. **Start filling the purse.** Save a tenth of your income.
2. **Control your spending.** Separate what is necessary from what you have
   come to count as necessary. Spending always grows to meet income unless you
   stop it.
3. **Make the money work.** Money sitting still does nothing. Returns that are
   reinvested keep working on their own.
4. **Guard against loss.** The principal comes first. Seek advice from people
   who actually know what you are asking about – not from those selling you
   something.
5. **Make your home a profitable investment.** The advice is to own, not rent.
6. **Ensure a future income.** Plan for old age and for others becoming
   dependent on you.
7. **Increase your ability to earn.** Skills are the one investment no one can
   take from you.

## On debt

The book has its own story about a man who owes money in several places, and
it is surprisingly concrete: set aside a fixed share of income to pay down
debt, divide it among those you owe, and keep going until it is done. No drama,
no quick fix – just a fixed amount every month, and time.

    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Spread the repayment</b><small>Everyone you owe gets something each month, in proportion to what they are owed. Nobody is passed over.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Tell them what the plan is</b><small>The book puts weight on laying the plan out openly rather than hiding from it.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Keep at it until it is done</b><small>The same amount, every month, until the debt is gone. That is the whole method.</small></span></li>

  <figcaption>There is no quick fix here, and that is the point. What makes this different from simply paying as much as possible is that the saving is allowed to live on alongside – so that you have something to show for it when the debt is cleared, rather than standing at zero again.</figcaption>

## What has not survived a hundred years

The advice to own your home stood stronger in 1926 than it does today. It takes
no account of current house prices, interest rates, or of the fact that tying
all your equity up in a single property is itself a concentrated risk. The book
also takes no account of inflation as a phenomenon, and the returns in its
examples are consistently optimistic.

It is also worth saying that the Babylon setting is literary, not historical.
Clason used the scenery to make the advice memorable.

## Why it is still read

Because the advice is simple enough to remember, and because the hard part is
not understanding it. The hard part is actually doing it, every month, for many
years. That has not changed in a hundred years.
