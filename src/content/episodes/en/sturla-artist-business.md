Sturla has covered a lot of ground in the Norwegian music industry: X Factor, Idol, The
Voice, Melodi Grand Prix and a few years under contract with Sony Music. Today he works
for himself, aiming to play 100 concerts during 2026.

This episode has two halves. The first is how he got there. The second is what few
people talk about: how the money in the music business actually moves.

## Sony and the 360 deal

The breakthrough came by chance. After three years at his mother's house, experimenting
with music and working at a clothing shop, he met a producer at a band night in
Trondheim. The producer passed some simple demos to a contact who had just started at
Sony in Oslo. The next day – while Sturla was folding trousers at work – word came that
they wanted to sign him.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">A 360 deal covers nearly the whole career – except the part the artist earns most from.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>How he explains it:</b> it is not employment, but a combined publishing and production contract. The label takes responsibility for funding studio time, production and release.<br /><b>The exception:</b> live performance. That stays with the artist.<br /><b>In practice:</b> he moved to Oslo on a student loan, and kept the shop job there for a while to make ends meet.</p>
</figure>

He left Sony after a few years. His explanation is sober: a label fights to keep its own
staff, and a project that does not quite take off becomes harder to justify. He was also
unsure of his own musical direction.

And then the thing worth noting happened: it was **after** the deal ended that things
started to move. He streamed more, played more concerts than ever, and was A-listed on
NRK P3 twice in one year.

## The two tracks: Tono and Gramo

This is the most precise and useful part of the episode, and worth understanding even if
you will never make music.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Two rights in one song</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Tono</p>
      <h4>The work itself</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The lyrics and the melody</li>
        <li>Belongs to whoever wrote the song</li>
        <li>Pays out quarterly</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Gramo</p>
      <h4>The recording</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The recording itself – the master</li>
        <li>Belongs to whoever owns it, and the performers on it</li>
        <li>Pays out mainly once a year, in March</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>When a song is played on the radio, both must be paid: the writer through Tono, and the recording through Gramo. They are entirely separate tracks, and the same person may hold both – or only one. Which is why "who owns the song" rarely has a single answer.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">Gramo income splits into two equal halves.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Half</b> goes to the producer – whoever financed and owns the recording. Sturla deliberately keeps that role himself, because it is favourable.<br /><b>The other half</b> is shared between the performers on the recording under a role-code model – lead vocal, backing vocal, individual instrument – where the sum is split in proportion to the points each role earns.<br />Any business playing music commercially, from a café to a concert promoter, has to register and pay into both. As an artist, Sturla registers his own concerts in Tono's portal, with audience numbers, ticket revenue and which songs were played.</p>
</figure>

He adds a point about the system: Tono is state-owned, and the rules are the same for
everyone. In several other countries, the United States among them, competing commercial
bodies collect rights and sign artists with advances – which he thinks makes the system
less fair than the Norwegian one.

## What streaming actually pays

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">His estimate: around 0.006 kroner per play. About 60,000 kroner for a million.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>And a play counts</b> after a few seconds of listening.<br /><b>He is open about his own position:</b> for a Norwegian artist he does not stream badly – and there is still little to be had. The payment arrives via PayPal, where he also pays for his own Spotify subscription. By the end of the year there is not much left.</p>
</figure>

## Two ways of being paid for a gig

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Guarantee or door</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Guarantee</p>
      <h4>The promoter pays a fixed sum</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The same amount regardless of turnout</li>
        <li>Gives the promoter a reason to promote it</li>
        <li>Predictable for the artist</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Door split</p>
      <h4>The artist takes the tickets</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The artist keeps the ticket revenue</li>
        <li>The venue earns on bar and food</li>
        <li>The upside is the artist's – and so is the risk</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>He sometimes negotiates a hybrid: a guarantee combined with a share of the surplus, particularly at venues he is unsure will draw a crowd on their own. The work rarely arrives by itself – he still sends cold emails and rings promoters by hand. The easiest calls are to places where he already has a way in; the hardest are to venues with an artistic profile far from his own.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The smelter, the Porsche and the decision

Alongside the music, Sturla worked shifts for several years at a smelter producing
silicon – an element used in toothpaste, car bodies and cosmetics, among other things.
The plant runs around the clock with roughly 147 employees across three shifts.

Shift work paid well, with good supplements. Some of the money went on a green Porsche
Taycan, bought used for about 1.2 million. It was sold two years later for 850,000, with
70,000–80,000 of outstanding loan to cover – a loss he reckons could have been far
worse.

He released a song called "67", after the ordinary retirement age. It is about breaking
out of the treadmill, and about the feeling that the job was only something he did for
the payslip.

In January 2026 he left for the second and final time.

## The target

Once the fallback job is gone, you have to make it work.<br /><b>And the daily reality behind it:</b> a sole proprietorship, accounts in Fiken, and a morning routine he introduced because nothing arrives by itself – up by nine at the latest, plan the day.</p>

## The advice

Asked what he would say to others wanting to go for it, the answer is not what you might
expect from an episode otherwise about business:

> Explore the music and be interested in music first. Things did not really move for me
> until I had a grip on what I wanted to do musically. If everything you do is based on
> the things around the music – not the music itself – I think it gets harder. Then you
> are no longer the one making the artistic choices.

*The content reflects Sturla's own experience and assessment of the industry he works
in. Amounts and rates are his own estimates, not official figures from Tono, Gramo or
Creo.*
