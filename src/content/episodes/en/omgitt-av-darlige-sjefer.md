The third book in Erikson's series is about management seen from below. The subject
is about as recognisable as they come – most people have had at least one boss they
remember for the wrong reasons – and it is worth going through without reducing it
to personality types.

So we do it a little differently from the book: we look at what the management
*does* to people, not at what the boss is assumed to *be*. It is a more usable angle,
for the simple reason that you are never going to change your boss's personality,
whereas behaviour and circumstances can sometimes be moved.

## The forms bad management takes

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Five forms, and what they do</p>
  <table>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Form</th><th scope="col">What it looks like</th><th scope="col">What it does to people</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Absent</th><td>Does not reply, does not decide</td><td>Nothing gets resolved, and there is nothing concrete to complain about</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Micromanaging</th><td>Monitors, corrects, takes over</td><td>Read as distrust, whatever the intention</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Unpredictable</th><td>Changes direction, priority and mood</td><td>People stop taking initiative</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Conflict-avoidant</th><td>Lets bad behaviour stand</td><td>In practice, siding with whoever behaves worst</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Takes the credit</th><td>Passes off others' work as their own</td><td>Destroys loyalty faster than anything else</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Note that only one of the five is unpleasant in the moment. The other four are unpleasant over time, and that is a significant difference: the slow forms are the ones people put up with longest, and the ones they eventually leave over.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Why absence is often worst

This is the book's most useful point, and it is counterintuitive. Research on
destructive leadership suggests that the absent manager does at least as much damage
as the aggressive one. The reason lies not in the behaviour but in what it leaves
behind.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Why the absent manager is harder to deal with</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">The aggressive one</p>
      <h4>Has done something</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The act can be described</li>
        <li>It has a date and witnesses</li>
        <li>There are rules to measure it against</li>
        <li>Colleagues agree on what happened</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">The absent one</p>
      <h4>Has failed to do something</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>There is no incident to point at</li>
        <li>Each missing decision looks trivial on its own</li>
        <li>Your complaint sounds like impatience</li>
        <li>The damage is cumulative and invisible</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The left-hand column is more serious, but it is also *actionable* – it has a way into the system. The right-hand one has none. That is why people can spend years with an absent manager and still feel they have nothing to bring forward.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Micromanagement, and why it reinforces itself

The intention behind micromanagement is rarely distrust. It is often quality, or the
manager's own anxiety about being caught out. But the intention makes no difference
to how it is read, and the mechanism has an unpleasant property: it confirms itself.

<figure class="fig fig--cycle">
  <p class="fig__title">The loop that makes micromanagement worse over time</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__step">1</span><b>The manager corrects</b><small>Steps into a detail and changes it.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">2</span><b>You stop deciding</b><small>It is not worth making a call that gets overruled anyway.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">3</span><b>More decisions go upwards</b><small>You ask instead of settling it.</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">4</span><b>The manager's suspicion is confirmed</b><small>"They can't make a decision on their own."</small></li>
    <li><span class="fig__step">5</span><b>Control tightens</b><small>And round the loop goes again.</small></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The point of drawing it is that neither party experiences themselves as the cause. The manager sees a team that will not take responsibility. You see a manager who will not let go. Both are right about the observation and wrong about the order – and the only one who can break the loop without risk is the one with the most power.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Unpredictability, and what it costs

The unpredictable manager is often seen as less serious than the others, because
they can be perfectly pleasant much of the time. The cost is high all the same, and
it is specific.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">What disappears when the direction keeps shifting</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Planning</b><small>There is no point making a plan that will probably be replaced on Friday.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Initiative</b><small>Proposing something exposes you. Better to wait to be told.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Long-term work</b><small>Anything taking more than a few weeks becomes risky to start.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Honesty</b><small>When the reaction varies with the mood, people learn to read the mood instead of saying what they think.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The last point is the most dangerous for the organisation, and it is nearly impossible to spot from above: a manager who receives steadily less bad news may easily conclude that things are improving.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Avoiding conflict is not neutrality

Letting bad behaviour stand feels, to the manager, like staying out of it. To
everybody else it is a choice.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">When nobody intervenes, whoever behaves worst has set the norm.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>What is actually communicated:</b> not "I am taking no position", but "this is acceptable here".<br /><b>Who pays:</b> not the person behaving badly, but the colleagues who have to compensate, work around it or put up with it.<br /><b>Why it is so common:</b> confronting requires an uncomfortable conversation now, while the cost of not doing so arrives later and lands on somebody else.</p>
</figure>

## Why it happens at all

A large part of the explanation is how people become managers. Few seek out
management because they want to manage; they are promoted because they were good at
the craft.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">Two jobs that do not resemble each other</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">The one you were good at</p>
      <h4>The craft</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>You solve the task yourself</li>
        <li>The quality is your own</li>
        <li>You get to see the result</li>
        <li>You are measured on what you produce</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">The one you were promoted into</p>
      <h4>Management</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>You get others to solve it</li>
        <li>The quality is theirs, and it varies</li>
        <li>You give feedback, including the uncomfortable kind</li>
        <li>You prioritise on other people's behalf</li>
        <li>You are measured on something you only partly control</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>The best nurse, salesperson or developer has no training in anything in the right-hand column. That the transition goes badly is therefore not a personality problem – it is a training problem, and responsibility for it sits above the new manager, not with them.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the core of the **Peter principle** from 1969: in a hierarchy, people rise
to a level they cannot handle, and there they stay. It was written as satire, but it
points at something real.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">The mechanism behind the Peter principle</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>You do the job well</b><small>The performance is visible and documented.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>You get promoted</b><small>The reward for mastery is a different job.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Repeat until it stops</b><small>The promotions continue for as long as you master the work.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>There you remain</b><small>The job you cannot master yields no further promotions – and rarely a way back.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The uncomfortable part is how reasonable each individual step is. Nobody makes a bad decision along the way. The system promotes for skill in the previous job rather than the next one, and the result follows by itself.</figcaption>
</figure>

## A distinction worth making

The book, like the others in the series, tends to explain behaviour by what people
*are*. It is more useful to separate two things that look alike from the outside.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">A manager behaving badly, or a manager in an impossible job?</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Behaving badly</p>
      <h4>Signs</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Lacks training, gets no feedback themselves</li>
        <li>Has never been corrected</li>
        <li>Has room to act, but uses it poorly</li>
        <li>Others in the same role manage it</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">An impossible job</p>
      <h4>Signs</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Too many direct reports to follow up anyone</li>
        <li>Contradictory demands from above</li>
        <li>Responsibility without the authority to decide</li>
        <li>Their predecessor struggled with exactly the same thing</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>That last point in the right-hand column is the single best indicator you have: if it goes wrong for everyone who has held the post, the post is wrong, not the person. The distinction is not merely fair – it is practical, because the two are solved in completely different ways, and because there are far more in the right-hand group than people think.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What you can do yourself

The order here is not accidental. It runs from what costs little and often works, to
what costs more.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">The ladder, cheapest to most expensive</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Ask for something specific</b><small>"Could we have a fixed fifteen minutes each week" is far easier to get than "you need to be more available". The first is a proposal, the second is a verdict.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Write agreements down</b><small>Unpredictability loses much of its force when what was agreed is in writing. A short email summary after a verbal decision is enough.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Escalate without going around</b><small>Inform, do not bypass. The first can be explained afterwards. The second rarely can be repaired.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Use the machinery</b><small>Union representatives, safety representatives and HR exist precisely for this, and employee involvement is regulated by law in Norwegian working life.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Consider whether it is worth it</b><small>Bad management is among the best documented reasons people quit. Leaving is not weakness.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Note that the first three do not require the manager to change. They change the conditions around the interaction, and that is more or less the only thing you actually control.</figcaption>
</figure>

One further point about writing things down: the purpose is not to build a case. It
is to remove the disagreement about what was said, so that next time the conversation
can be about the substance. If it does later become a case, you have the
documentation – but that is a side effect, not the aim.

## About the book, and the series

Erikson writes in a readable, recognisable way, and his strength is that he puts
words to something people recognise. The weakness is the same as in the rest of the
series: the explanations lean heavily on personality types, and the model he builds
on has drawn substantial criticism for a weak scientific basis. That is covered in
more detail in the episode on *Surrounded by Idiots*.

For this particular subject it matters less than you might think, because the most
useful thing in the book is not the typology. It is the observation that absence is
a form of bad management, and that it is the most common one.

*This is a general discussion of a topic, not employment-law advice on a specific
case. If you are in a serious situation, contact a union representative, a safety
representative or a lawyer.*
