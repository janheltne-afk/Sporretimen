The third book in Erikson's series is about leadership seen from below. The
subject is among the most recognisable there is – most people have had at least
one boss they remember for the wrong reasons – and it is worth going through
without reducing it to personality types.

## The forms bad leadership takes

- **Absent.** Does not reply, does not decide, is never available when it is
  urgent. This is probably the most common form, and research on destructive
  leadership suggests it does at least as much damage as the aggressive kind –
  because nothing gets dealt with, and because there is nothing concrete to
  complain about.
- **Micromanaging.** Monitors, corrects and takes over. The intention is often
  quality or the manager's own anxiety, but it always reads as distrust.
- **Unpredictable.** Changes direction, priority and mood. That makes planning
  impossible, and people stop taking initiative.
- **Conflict-avoidant.** Lets bad behaviour stand unchallenged, which in
  practice means siding with whoever behaves worst.
- **The one who takes the credit.** Passes on others' work as their own.
  Destroys loyalty faster than almost anything else.

## Why it happens

A large part of the explanation is how people become managers. Very few seek out
leadership because they want to lead; they are promoted because they were good
at the craft. But the best nurse, salesperson or developer has no training in
giving feedback, handling conflict or prioritising on other people's behalf.

This is the core of the **Peter principle** from 1969: in a hierarchy, people
rise until they reach a level they cannot handle, and there they stay. It was
written as satire, but it points at something real – we promote for skills in
the previous job, not the next one.

## A distinction worth making

The book, like the others in the series, tends to explain behaviour by what
people *are*. It is more usable to distinguish between:

- **A boss who acts badly.** Lacks training, gets no feedback themselves, has
  never been corrected.
- **A boss in an impossible situation.** Too many reports, contradictory demands
  from above, no authority over what they are held responsible for.

The second looks like the first from outside, but cannot be solved the same way.
And there are far more people in the second group than the first.

## What you can do yourself

- **Ask for something specific.** “Can we have a fixed fifteen minutes each
  week” is easier to get than “you need to be more available”.
- **Write agreements down.** Unpredictability loses much of its force when what
  was agreed is written.
- **Deliver upwards without going around.** Inform, do not bypass. The latter
  cannot be repaired.
- **Use the machinery.** Safety representatives, union representatives and HR
  exist precisely for this, and employee participation is regulated by law in
  Norwegian working life.
- **Consider whether it is worth it.** Bad leadership is one of the
  best-documented reasons people quit. Leaving is not weakness.

*The content is a general walk through a subject, not employment-law advice on a
specific case. If you are in a serious situation, contact a union or safety
representative, or a lawyer.*
