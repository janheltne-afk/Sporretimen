**Ray Dalio** founded Bridgewater Associates in 1975, from an apartment in New
York. It became the world's largest hedge fund. *Principles* is his attempt to
write down how he and the firm make decisions – first for employees, then for
everyone.

The book is in three parts: his own story, principles for life, and principles
for work. The last two are lists – hundreds of numbered points. The form is the
point.

## Why write them down

The premise is simple. Most situations you end up in, you have been in before,
in some variation. If you have to work out from scratch what to do every time,
you make worse decisions than if you had thought it through once, calmly, and
written down the answer.

A **principle**, then, is a rule you have made for yourself for a kind of
situation that recurs. Dalio's view is that everyone has such rules – most
people have just never written them down, and so do not quite know what they
are.

## Pain + reflection = progress

The most quoted formula in the book. Mistakes hurt, and that is fine – it is
the signal that something is worth learning from. But the pain by itself gives
you nothing. It is the reflection afterwards that moves you forward.

Bridgewater took this literally, with systems in which employees logged
mistakes and were asked to analyse them in the open. This is where the book
starts to get controversial.

## Radical truthfulness and radical transparency

Dalio's idea is that most organisations make bad decisions because people do
not say what they think. Disagreement is swept under the carpet, the boss is
right because they are the boss, and the problems sit there until they are
large.

His answer is to invert it: everyone says what they think, to everyone, and
meetings are recorded so anyone can see what was said. It sounds brutal, and by
many employees' accounts it was.

## Idea meritocracy and believability weighting

The core of the work principles. The best idea should win – not the one from
the person with the highest rank, and not the one with the most votes either.

To get there, Bridgewater weights people's opinions by **believability**: what
have you achieved before, in this specific area? Someone who has been right
about something many times counts for more than someone who has never tried.
It is a more honest way of saying what most organisations do quietly.

## The five-step process

The practical core, usable independently of the rest:

1. **Set clear goals.**
2. **Identify the problems** standing in their way. Do not tolerate them.
3. **Diagnose** what is actually causing them – not the symptom.
4. **Design a plan** to remove the cause.
5. **Execute.**

The point is that people are good at different steps, and that most skip
step 3.

## The criticism

Bridgewater's culture has been described by former employees as everything
from liberating to psychologically punishing, and the firm has had notably
high turnover among new hires. Much of what worked there worked because Dalio
himself was there and had the authority to hold it up. It is far from certain
it transplants.

The book is also long, repetitive, and bears the marks of an author who has
been right about a great deal and tells you so. Dalio's macroeconomic
predictions in the years since have had a mixed record.

## What is worth taking with you

The idea of writing down your own decision rules is useful for almost anyone,
and requires no hedge fund. The same goes for the five-step process, and for
the idea that disagreement left unspoken costs more than disagreement voiced.
The rest is worth reading as a description of one particular place, not as a
recipe.
