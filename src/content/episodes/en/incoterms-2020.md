Few things in international trade cause as much argument as three letters in a
contract. **FOB**, **CIF**, **DAP** – the abbreviations are used daily, and
they are often used wrongly.

This episode walks through all eleven rules in **Incoterms® 2020**, what each
one actually says about who carries what, and where it tends to go wrong.

## First: what the rules do not do

This is the most important section in the episode, and the one most often
skipped.

  <p class="fig__title">The reach</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">The rules allocate</p>
      <h4>Four things</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>Who does what – transport, customs clearance, documents</li>
        <li>Who pays for what</li>
        <li>Where the risk for the goods passes from seller to buyer</li>
        <li>Who, if anyone, has to insure</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">The rules say nothing about</p>
      <h4>Everything else</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>When title to the goods passes</li>
        <li>The price, the payment and the payment terms</li>
        <li>What happens on breach or delay</li>
        <li>Which country's law applies, and where disputes are settled</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Incoterms are not a contract of sale, but a building block in one. That the risk passes in Rotterdam does not mean the buyer owns the goods in Rotterdam – transfer of title is governed by the contract and by the law applying to it. A great many disputes start right here.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Two families

The rules are split into two groups, and that split matters more than most
people think.

**Seven rules apply to any mode of transport** – road, rail, air, sea, or
several in combination: EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU and DDP.

**Four rules apply to sea and inland waterway transport only**: FAS, FOB, CFR
and CIF. They were made for goods loaded over the rail or alongside the ship –
bulk, break-bulk, project cargo. Not containers.

## All eleven

    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">EXW</th><td>Ex Works</td><td>At the seller's, when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal. Not loaded.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">FCA</th><td>Free Carrier</td><td>When the goods are handed to the carrier the buyer has nominated</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">CPT</th><td>Carriage Paid To</td><td>On handover to the first carrier – not on arrival</td></tr>

      <tr><th scope="row">DAP</th><td>Delivered at Place</td><td>On arrival, ready for unloading. The seller does not unload.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">DPU</th><td>Delivered at Place Unloaded</td><td>When the goods have been unloaded at the named place</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">DDP</th><td>Delivered Duty Paid</td><td>As DAP, but the seller also handles import clearance and duties</td></tr>

</figure>

## The staircase from E to D

The first letter is not arbitrary. It tells you how much of the job the
seller has taken on.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">From least to most seller obligation</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>E — EXW</b><small>The seller makes the goods ready at their own premises, and does no more. The buyer carries most.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>F — FCA, FAS, FOB</b><small>The seller delivers to the carrier the buyer has arranged.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>C — CPT, CIP, CFR, CIF</b><small>The seller pays the carriage onward, but does not carry the risk there.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>D — DAP, DPU, DDP</b><small>The seller carries the risk all the way to the destination.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The C group is the one that catches people out. It looks like a delivery term to the destination, because the seller pays the freight there – but the risk left the seller back at departure.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The two critical points

This is the heart of the C rules, and the single misunderstanding that costs
the most money.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">CIF Rotterdam, seen from each point</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">The cost point</p>
      <h4>Rotterdam</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The seller pays freight and insurance to here</li>
        <li>This is what the contract says</li>
        <li>This is what people remember</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">The risk point</p>
      <h4>The port of departure</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The risk passed when the goods came on board</li>
        <li>If something goes wrong en route, it is the buyer's loss</li>
        <li>The buyer claims under the insurance, not against the seller</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Two places, one rule. In the E, F and D rules the two coincide; in the C rules they never do. If you remember one thing from this episode, make it this one.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The container mistake

The most common error in practice, and an easy one to avoid once you know about
it.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">If the goods travel in a container, FOB is almost always the wrong rule. Use FCA.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Why:</b> FOB moves the risk when the goods are on board the vessel. A container is delivered to a terminal, often days before it is loaded, and the seller loses control the moment it is handed over there. Under FOB the seller still carries the risk for a container they can neither see nor touch. FCA moves the risk to where the handover actually happens. The same applies in pairs: CFR and CIF should be CPT and CIP.</p>
</figure>

This is not a technicality. The combination of “FOB” and “container” appears in
a great many contracts, and ICC has pointed out across several editions of the
rules that it is a misuse.

## What changed in 2020

The changes from 2010 to 2020 are not dramatic, but some of them have practical
consequences.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">2010 → 2020</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">The change</th><th scope="col">What it means in practice</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">DAT became DPU</th><td>The name now states the decisive part: delivered <i>unloaded</i>. And the rule is no longer tied to a terminal – the place can be anywhere.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">CIP requires full cover</th><td>The seller must now insure at the highest standard level. CIF stayed on minimum cover – so the two are no longer alike on this point.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">FCA and the on-board bill of lading</th><td>The parties can agree that the buyer instructs the carrier to issue an on-board bill of lading to the seller. Solves an old problem for anyone drawing on a letter of credit.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Own means of transport</th><td>The rules now allow for a party carrying the goods themselves, rather than assuming a third-party carrier.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Security requirements</th><td>Obligations and costs tied to security screening are allocated more clearly.</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Costs in one place</th><td>All costs for each party are now listed together, instead of scattered through the rule.</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Older versions do not stop applying. If the parties agree on “Incoterms 2010”, that is what governs between them. Which is precisely why the year has to be in the contract.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Three traps

**DDP is easier to promise than to deliver.** The seller takes on import
clearance and all duties in the buyer's country. In many countries a foreign
seller cannot act as importer of record without registering, and VAT is not
always recoverable. What was meant as good service becomes a cost nobody
budgeted.

**EXW sits badly across borders.** The seller has no obligation to clear for
export or to load. The buyer is then meant to arrange export from a country
they may have no presence in, and needs documentation only the seller holds.
ICC itself points out that the rule suits domestic trade best. If you want
something similar, FCA is usually the better fit.

**Insurance is minimum unless otherwise agreed.** Under CIF the seller's duty
is minimum cover, and it covers less than most people assume. If the buyer
wants more, it has to be agreed separately. Under CIP this changed in 2020, but
under CIF it stands.

## How to write it properly

A delivery term missing one of three elements is a dispute waiting to happen.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Three elements, all necessary</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>1. The rule</b><small>The three letters. Pick the one that fits the mode of transport – not the one you have always used.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>2. The place, as precisely as possible</b><small>Not just “Oslo”, but which terminal, which gate, which address. This is where the risk changes hands.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>3. The version</b><small>“Incoterms 2020”. Without the year it is unclear which edition applies.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Written out it becomes, for example, “FCA Gardermoen Cargo Terminal, Oslo, Incoterms 2020”. The imprecise place is the most common omission – “DAP Norway” says nothing about where the seller's risk actually ends.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Why this is worth knowing

The delivery term is three letters in a contract, and it decides who pays when
the container sits waiting, when the vessel runs late, when the goods arrive
damaged, and who has to deal with the customs authorities in a country they do
not know. It also affects the price: a price “DDP Oslo” and a price “FCA
Shanghai” are not comparable until you have costed everything in between.

Which is why three letters are worth half an hour.

## If you want to go deeper

If you are going to use the rules in practice, there is Norwegian-language
material on them worth knowing about.

**Christian J. Lien** is behind the Norwegian editions of the rules: he
initiated, translated and published *Incoterms 1990*, *Incoterms 2000* and
*Incoterms® 2010* in Norwegian, and translated *Incoterms® 2020* into Norwegian
for ICC Norway in 2020.

He has also written and published two commentary editions – **Norsk Kommentar
til Incoterms® 2010** and **Norsk Kommentar til Incoterms® 2020** – working
through the rules in Norwegian in far more depth than an episode can. The
latter runs to 312 pages.

He has taught and lectured on the subject for 35 years, both open and in-house
courses, for organisations including Innovasjon Norge, Logistikkforeningen,
NIMA, ICC Norway and BI Norwegian Business School. If you need to go further
than this episode reaches – and especially if it concerns a specific contract –
that is where to go. His site is [cjlien.no](https://cjlien.no/). Note that the
commentary editions are in Norwegian.
