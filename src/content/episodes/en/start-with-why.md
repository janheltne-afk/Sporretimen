**Simon Sinek** gave a TED talk in 2009 that has become one of the most watched
ever, and *Start With Why* is the long version. At its core is an observation
about order.

## The golden circle

Three rings. On the outside, **what** you do – the thing everyone can answer. In
the middle, **how** you do it – what sets you apart. At the centre, **why** you
do it – the purpose, the belief, the reason the organisation exists beyond
making money.

Sinek's claim is that almost everyone communicates from the outside in: here is
the product, here are the features, buy it. The few who inspire do the opposite.
They begin at the centre.

His example is Apple. The ordinary version: “We make great computers.
Beautifully designed, easy to use. Want to buy one?” The inverted one: “We
believe in challenging the status quo. The way we do it is by making products
that are beautifully designed and simple to use. We happen to make computers
too.” Same information, entirely different effect.

The line that sums it up: **people don't buy what you do, they buy why you do
it.**

## Manipulation or inspiration

Sinek draws a sharp line. **Manipulation** is discounts, promotions, fear, peer
pressure, novelty. It works – but only this time, and it has to be repeated and
usually increased next time. **Inspiration** is when people act because they
share your belief, and then they do not need persuading again.

The distinction is practical: if sales stop the moment you stop promoting, you
never had loyalty.

## The early adopters

Sinek borrows the diffusion curve from Everett Rogers: innovators, early
adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards. His point is that you do not
win the majority directly. You win the 15–18 per cent on the left – those who
act on belief rather than evidence – and they pull the rest along. The mass
market buys because others went first.

## The celery test

The most usable device in the book. Imagine many people advise you on what to
do: buy Oreos, buy rice, buy celery, buy M&M's. Buy everything, and nobody can
see what you stand for. Know why you are there – you eat healthily – and you buy
the celery and the rice, and it is visible from the pavement what you are up to.

The point is that a clear why is first of all a filter for what to say no to.

## What is questionable

This has to be included. Sinek grounds the golden circle biologically: that
*what* addresses the neocortex, which handles language and rational analysis,
while *why* addresses the limbic system, which governs feeling and
decision-making – and that the limbic system has no language, which is why good
choices feel right without our being able to explain them.

That is a heavy simplification. The brain does not divide labour that way, and
the split into an “emotional brain” and a “thinking brain” is a model
specialists have abandoned. The argument gives the book a scientific wrapping it
has no warrant for.

The examples are also chosen after the fact. Apple, Southwest and
Harley-Davidson succeeded, and the purpose is then read into the explanation –
while companies with an equally clear why that went under are not mentioned.
Several of the businesses Sinek held up have had hard years since 2009.

And most of the content is in the eighteen-minute TED talk. The book is largely
the same thought, repeated.

## What holds up

The advice itself. That people join a reason, not a feature list. That knowing
why you do something makes it easier to say no. And that a message which has to
be bought with a discount every time is not a message. You can take that away
regardless of what happens in the limbic system.

*The content is a walk through what the book argues, together with the criticism
it has met.*
