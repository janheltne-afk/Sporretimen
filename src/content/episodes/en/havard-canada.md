In the summer of 2025 Håvard Hasund and four others paddled **1,500 kilometres**
through the wilderness of northern Canada – from Yellowknife to Baker Lake, in 47
days, without seeing another person on the way.

This is the whole trip: how it came about, what could have gone wrong, and what
actually happened.

## Why Canada

No single person came up with the trip. The group had been talking about Canada for
years, and the inspiration came from two directions: **Helge Ingstad** and his book
*Pelsjegerliv*, written after three years in this very area, and **Lars Monsen**, from
whom they had also taken concrete tips.

The reason it had to be Canada is simple: continuous wilderness on a scale that does
not exist in Norway. Håvard reckons Russia is the only comparable country, and going
there is difficult now.

He adds an observation: there are strikingly many Norwegians in that particular area.
Had Ingstad not written his book, few of them would have been there.

## Planning starts with a date

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">You have to set a date, and then build from that date.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>How it happened:</b> at Christmas 2023 the group sat over a beer and the decision was made – summer 2025, whatever happens. That left a year and a half to save money, plan the route and gather kit.<br /><b>Why then:</b> this was something they wanted to do before children and family made it harder.<br /><b>How:</b> everything lived in a single spreadsheet, with separate tabs for budget, menu and equipment. Planning happened in fits and starts across the whole period, not in intense sessions. Much of the kit they already had – you take essentially what you would take on a two-week trip in the Norwegian mountains.</p>

## Two groups in one party

This is the most important structural detail of the trip, and it comes back more than
once.

They did not travel together. Håvard and one other flew to Yellowknife and paddled
**alone for two weeks** before the other three were flown in. That made two separate
groups, each with its own budget, travel planning, equipment and calorie budget.

The group wanted to be five at most. Five does not divide into pairs, and they did not
want a third canoe – so it became two in one and three in the other. Whether three in
one canoe would even work in rapids and heavy waves was something they were unsure
about for a long time. They tested it in Oslo beforehand. It worked; it was just
cramped.

## The biggest risk

Håvard is a paramedic, and got the question many people wonder about: how do you think
about risk when help is hours away? The answer is sober – you cannot know that it will
be fine.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">The dangerous thing is not the animals. It is capsizing into four-degree water.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>The arithmetic:</b> in a capsize, hypothermia is the real threat, and Håvard's estimate is that you have around <b>ten minutes</b>.<br /><b>Where it came closest:</b> in several places they crossed 3–4 kilometres of open water to avoid paddling in and out of long bays. They assessed the wind and waited when it blew too hard, but paddling around was never an option. One crossing they had postponed until evening because of wind – and ended up crossing in the middle of the night when it suddenly picked up.</p>
</figure>

On the emergency beacon he is equally sober:

> It takes many hours before it gets there, so you have to know what to do until then.

Against bears the measures were practical. Bear spray on everyone, and a scent barrier
around camp – they always walked a wide circle around the site and urinated, so the
bears would not come too close.

## Food to the gram

Two days in Yellowknife went on logistics: food, lures and ammunition. They spent a
whole evening with a scale and a calorie budget, packing breakfast and nut rations to
the gram for about 40 days.

Breakfast was **porridge with powdered milk, sugar and rapeseed oil**, every single
day. The recipe comes from the polar explorer Børge Ousland, and the verdict is clear:
excellent.

The first two weeks were another story. The pair who paddled first bought ordinary
supermarket food – bacon, steak and cheese – in a rush right before departure. On day
four the steak tasted odd, and the rest was thrown out. They had started eating fish
anyway.

## The portage, solved two ways

The first job once the party came together was to move all the equipment **five
kilometres and 200 metres of ascent**. The two groups solved it differently, without
having agreed on anything.

## When they lost each other

Shortly after joining up came the episode that led to the only real conversation within
the group.

The three carried faster than the two and set off before the others were ready. Soon
they were a kilometre ahead – at which point they are impossible to catch. Heading up a
river they chose a route that looked wrong on the map. Håvard and his partner shouted,
were not heard, paddled up the river and could not find them. After contact on inReach
it turned out they were just on the other side of the headland.

*The route the three chose turned out, incidentally, to be the better one – a narrow
channel that ran all the way to the next lake, saving them a carry.*

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">We have to wait for each other.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Why this is not a courtesy rule:</b> two canoes are the safety margin. If one capsizes, the other is the only rescue. A kilometre's lead is enough for that rescue not to exist – and in four-degree water the margin is ten minutes. The conclusion was raised directly within the group, and is the most important safety lesson in the episode.</p>
</figure>

## The shotgun that disappeared

On the fourth day after joining up, one of the three shot at a hare from the canoe.
During a hurried reload the shotgun slid over the gunwale and vanished into the water.
They marked the position on GPS immediately.

Håvard free-dives, and measured the depth with a water-filled bottle and five metres of
rope. It stopped at around four metres, which seemed manageable. He stripped off,
paddled out and dived. His dive watch read **seven metres**, and it was clearly a good
deal deeper than that.

The shotgun is still in a nameless lake in northern Canada. Håvard has the coordinates.

## The rapids

Asked what the single best thing about the trip was, the answer is rapids.

He describes the first one: the big canoe portaged past, since three people in a longer
canoe is far less stable. Håvard and his partner paddled down, pulled into an eddy –
where you turn the canoe against the current and sit completely still – and studied the
river below. The plan was to get across to the left and avoid some large waves. They
tried, almost made it, and realised it would not work. So they simply had to drive
straight out into them.

> It went completely fine. But bloody hell, it was fun.

## Day 40

After around 35–36 days the two groups separated again. Håvard and his partner had had
their fill and wanted to get home; the other three wanted to use the time they had.
They parted amicably – and it is after this that the trip's densest wildlife encounters
come.

## Baker Lake

After 47 days in the wilderness they went straight to the shopping centre. There was a
Pizza Hut and a Tim Hortons – three counters, but the same people serving all three.
The Pizza Hut was closed, so it was a burger.

It was cold. It was also magnificent.

Then they bought white bread, mayonnaise, ham, cheese, crisps, Snickers and a great
deal of sweet pastry, and lay in the hotel room for nearly three days watching Netflix
on a phone.

*Håvard brought two pairs of boxer shorts on the trip. One pair was worn for 53 days.*
