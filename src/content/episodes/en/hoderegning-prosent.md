Percentages are the arithmetic people use most outside school, and get wrong
most. Not because the sums are hard, but because two or three of the techniques
are never said out loud – and because a couple of the traps look like the right
answer.

## Ten per cent is the key

Almost all mental percentage work starts in the same place: move the decimal
point one step and you have ten per cent. The rest is building.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">Everything built from ten per cent of 640</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>10% = 64</b><small>Move the point one step left. This is the whole foundation.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>5% = 32</b><small>Half of ten per cent.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>20% = 128</b><small>Double ten per cent.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>15% = 96</b><small>Ten plus five: 64 + 32.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>1% = 6.4</b><small>Move the point two steps. Useful for the awkward percentages.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>35% = 224</b><small>Three tens and a five: 64 + 64 + 64 + 32.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>Note that none of the steps is a multiplication. It is moving a decimal point, halving, and a little addition. That is the whole reason percentages suit mental arithmetic: they can be done without multiplying at all, as long as you build from the ten.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Percentages can be turned around

This is the trick that saves the most time, and it is almost unknown.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">x per cent of y is always the same as y per cent of x. If the sum faces the wrong way, turn it round.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>8% of 50</b> looks like something you have to calculate. Turned round it is <b>50% of 8</b> – half of 8, which is <b>4</b>.<br /><b>4% of 75</b> → <b>75% of 4</b> = three quarters of 4 = <b>3</b>.<br /><b>18% of 50</b> → <b>50% of 18</b> = <b>9</b>.<br />It works because both sides are the same product divided by a hundred. Order does not matter in a multiplication, and a percentage is a multiplication.</p>
</figure>

The rule for when to turn it round is simple: turn it if the other number is
easier to take a percentage of. And 50, 25 and 10 always are.

## Discounts: work out what you pay

The common mistake here is not mathematical, but unnecessary work.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">Two routes to the same price</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Discount</th><th scope="col">The long way</th><th scope="col">The short way</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">30% off 450</th><td>find 135, subtract from 450</td><td>pay 70%: 315</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">20% off 899</th><td>find 179.80, subtract</td><td>pay 80%: 719.20</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">25% off 320</th><td>find 80, subtract</td><td>pay 75%: 240</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>Both columns give the same answer, but the right-hand one has one step fewer – and one step fewer is half the work when the work is two steps. If all you want is roughly what something costs, "pay 70 per cent" gives you the number directly.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Value added tax

This is the trap most people fall into, and it looks entirely right.

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">To remove 25 per cent VAT you cannot subtract 25 per cent. You have to multiply by 0.8.</p>
  <p class="fig__example"><b>Forwards:</b> 500 before VAT becomes 500 × 1.25 = <b>625</b> with VAT.<br /><b>Back, correctly:</b> 625 × 0.8 = <b>500</b>.<br /><b>Back, wrongly:</b> 625 − 25% of 625 = 625 − 156.25 = <b>468.75</b>. That is 31 too little.<br />The reason is that the 25 per cent was calculated on the <i>net</i> price, while you are now subtracting it from the <i>gross</i> price – a bigger number. The percentage is the same, but the base has been swapped.</p>
</figure>

Which leads to a figure worth knowing by heart:

<figure class="fig fig--rule">
  <p class="fig__claim">In a price carrying 25 per cent VAT, the tax is 20 per cent of the total – one fifth.</p>
  <p class="fig__example">Of 625, <b>125</b> is VAT. That is a fifth of 625, not a quarter. If you need the tax inside a price, divide by 5. If you need the price without it, multiply by 0.8 – or subtract the fifth, which is the same calculation.</p>
</figure>

## Per cent and percentage points

This is not pedantry. They are two different numbers, and the gap is often
large.

<figure class="fig fig--compare">
  <p class="fig__title">A rate goes from 4 per cent to 6 per cent</p>
  <div class="fig__cols">
    <div class="fig__col">
      <p class="fig__lead">Percentage points</p>
      <h4>2 percentage points</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The difference between the two percentages</li>
        <li>6 − 4 = 2</li>
        <li>Used when comparing two percentages</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
    <div class="fig__col" data-accent>
      <p class="fig__lead">Per cent</p>
      <h4>A rise of 50 per cent</h4>
      <ul>
        <li>The change measured against where it started</li>
        <li>2 divided by 4 = a half</li>
        <li>This is what your interest bill actually does</li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </div>
  <figcaption>Both numbers are correct, and they describe the same event. Which is exactly why confusing them is useful to anyone wanting to overstate or understate: "only two percentage points" and "fifty per cent more expensive" are the same rate rise. When you read a figure about a change in something that is itself measured in per cent, it is worth checking which of the two is on the page.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Up and down are not symmetrical

This surprises most people the first time, and it has practical consequences.

<figure class="fig fig--flow" data-dir="ned">
  <p class="fig__title">100, up 25 per cent and down 25 per cent</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Start: 100</b><small>The starting point.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Up 25% → 125</b><small>The 25 per cent is taken of 100, and comes to 25.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>Down 25% → 93.75</b><small>Now the 25 per cent is taken of 125, and comes to 31.25. The fall is bigger than the rise was.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>You do not lose money because of the mathematics, but because the second percentage has a bigger base than the first. To get back to 100 from 125 you need to fall <b>20</b> per cent, not 25. The rule is that a rise of p per cent is undone by a fall of p divided by 100 plus p – for 25 that is 25/125, or 20 per cent.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Growth factors

The way out of this mess is to stop adding and subtracting, and start
multiplying.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">The change as a single number</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Change</th><th scope="col">Multiply by</th><th scope="col">Note</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">Up 25%</th><td>1.25</td><td>adds a quarter</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Down 20%</th><td>0.8</td><td>the counterpart to 1.25</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Up 10%</th><td>1.1</td><td></td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Up 10% twice</th><td>1.21</td><td>that is 21 per cent, not 20</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Down 20%, then up 25%</th><td>1.0</td><td>back where you started</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">Up 50%, then down 50%</th><td>0.75</td><td>25 per cent below the start</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The advantage is that several changes in a row simply multiply together, in any order. The fourth row is why "we grew ten per cent two years running" is not twenty per cent: the second year you grow off a bigger number. The bottom row is worth remembering the next time something falls fifty per cent and rises fifty per cent again.</figcaption>
</figure>

## The rule of 72

An estimate, not a formula – but a good estimate, and the only thing you need to
judge a rate of return in your head.

<figure class="fig fig--matrix">
  <p class="fig__title">72 divided by the rate gives the years to double</p>
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th scope="col">Rate</th><th scope="col">The rule says</th><th scope="col">Actual factor after those years</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><th scope="row">3%</th><td>24 years</td><td>2.03</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">4%</th><td>18 years</td><td>2.03</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">6%</th><td>12 years</td><td>2.01</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">8%</th><td>9 years</td><td>2.00</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">10%</th><td>7.2 years</td><td>1.99</td></tr>
      <tr><th scope="row">12%</th><td>6 years</td><td>1.97</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
  <figcaption>The right-hand column is what the money is actually multiplied by after the number of years the rule gives. Between 3 and 12 per cent it lands within a few per cent, and it is most precise around 8. Outside that range it gets worse, and it says nothing about what happens if the rate changes along the way.</figcaption>
</figure>

## Working backwards

The last case: you know the percentage and the result, but not what you started
with.

<figure class="fig fig--flow">
  <p class="fig__title">Three backwards questions</p>
  <ol>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>35 is 70% of what?</b><small>Divide by the growth factor: 35 ÷ 0.7 = 50. Or reason that if 70% is 35, then 10% is 5, and 100% is 50.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>What percentage is 18 of 24?</b><small>18 divided by 24 is three quarters, so 75%.</small></span></li>
    <li><span class="fig__box"><b>What percentage is 45 of 360?</b><small>45 divided by 360 is an eighth, so 12.5%.</small></span></li>
  </ol>
  <figcaption>The bottom two are why it pays to know the fractions as percentages – then the answer is recognition rather than calculation. The first is the reason to think in growth factors: going forwards you multiply, going back you divide by the same number.</figcaption>
</figure>

## What actually needs practising

**Ten per cent, and the four steps from it.** Half, double, ten plus five, and
the point two steps over. With those, you have covered most of what you meet.

**The fractions as percentages.** A quarter is 25, an eighth is 12.5, a third is
33⅓. They are covered in [the episode on
division](/en/episodes/hoderegning-deling/), and they turn backwards work into
recognition.

**0.8 and 1.25.** These two numbers cover VAT in both directions, and they are
the same number turned upside down. Know them and you take a price there and
back without thinking.

And then the one habit worth most: always ask **per cent of what**. The two
traps in this episode – VAT backwards and up-and-down – are the same mistake
both times. The percentage was right. The base had been swapped.
