**Chris Voss** was the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator. When he
later taught at Harvard, he found that the techniques from hostage cases beat
the ones taught on negotiation courses. *Never Split the Difference* (2016,
written with Tahl Raz) is the result.

## Why not meet in the middle

The title is the whole point. If you want black shoes and your partner wants
brown, the compromise is one of each – an outcome neither of you wanted. Voss
argues that compromise is usually what we reach for because it feels fair and
is easy, not because it is good. The alternative is to work out what the other
side *actually* needs.

## Tactical empathy

The core concept. Empathy here does not mean agreeing or being nice – it means
understanding what the other person feels and why, and showing that you have
understood it. People who feel understood lower their guard. The tools are few
and concrete:

- **Mirroring.** Repeat the last few words the other person said, as a
  question. It makes people elaborate, almost without noticing.
- **Labelling.** Put words to what the other person seems to feel: “It sounds
  like you're worried about …”. Right or wrong – they will correct you, and you
  have learned something.
- **The accusation audit.** Say the worst they might think of you before they
  say it themselves. It takes the sting out.

## Calibrated questions

Open questions beginning with *how* or *what*, which cannot be answered yes or
no. The most important of them: **“How am I supposed to do that?”** It moves the
problem to the other side of the table without saying no, and makes them treat
your problem as theirs.

## No is a beginning

Conventional negotiation hunts for yes. Voss argues the opposite: a **no** gives
people a sense of control and safety, and only then does the real conversation
begin. An early yes is often just politeness.

What he hunts for instead is **“that's right”** – the moment the other person
feels you have summarised their situation correctly. That is something quite
different from “you're right”, which is what people say when they want you to
stop talking.

## The Ackerman model

For straight price negotiations the book has a recipe: decide your target, open
at 65 per cent of it, and move up in shrinking steps – 85, 95, 100 – with an
odd final number and ideally something non-monetary at the end. The point is
that shrinking steps signal you are approaching your limit.

## The criticism

The book is anecdotal – stories from hostage cases, not studies. Several people
have pointed out that a kidnapping and a salary negotiation are rather different
situations, and that what works when the other party is desperate does not
necessarily work against a procurement manager who has read the same book.

Voss also cites the so-called 7-38-55 rule – that only 7 per cent of
communication is words – which is a widespread misreading of Albert Mehrabian's
research from the 1960s. The rule applies to a narrow experiment, not to
communication in general.

What is worth taking anyway is the attitude: that the aim is to understand, that
questions beat arguments, and that a no is not the end.
