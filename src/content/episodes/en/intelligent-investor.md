**Benjamin Graham** taught investing at Columbia University, and among his
students sat a young Warren Buffett. Buffett has since called *The Intelligent
Investor* the best book on investing ever written. It came out in 1949, and the
most widely used editions are the revised one from 1973.

The book is heavy going and full of calculations that are out of date. But
three of its ideas have become so central that they are used by people who have
never opened it.

## Investing or speculating

Graham starts with a definition stricter than the one most people use: an
**investment** presupposes thorough analysis, protection of the principal and a
reasonable expected return. Anything that does not meet all three is
**speculation**.

It is not a moral judgement – Graham thinks speculation can be fine, as long as
you know that is what you are doing and have set aside a sum you can afford to
lose. The mistake is speculating while believing you are investing.

## Mr. Market

The best-known image in the book. Imagine you own a small part of a business
together with a partner called Mr. Market. Every day he drops by and gives you
a price: he will buy your share, or sell you his. The price swings wildly,
because he is extremely moody. Some days he is euphoric, other days despairing.

The point is what you do with it. You are under no obligation to trade. You can
say no thanks every single day. And you can use him: buy when he is despairing,
sell when he is euphoric. What you should **not** do is let his mood decide
what you believe the business is worth.

## Margin of safety

Graham's own answer to what investing boils down to, if he had to pick three
words: *margin of safety*. Pay clearly less than what you believe something is
worth.

The gap is not a bonus. It is there because your analysis may be wrong,
because the future may disappoint, and because unexpected things happen. The
margin is what lets you keep going when you are wrong – and you will be,
sometimes.

The same idea is why he warns against paying too much for growth. A good
business can be a bad investment if the price already assumes everything goes
well.

## Defensive or enterprising

Graham divides investors in two. The **defensive** investor does not want to
spend time and effort following the market, and should therefore hold a
simple, broadly spread portfolio with fixed, regular purchases. The
**enterprising** investor is willing to put in substantial work, and can look
for mispricing – but must then actually do the work.

The key message is that there is no middle level. Half-hearted stock picking
gives you the costs of being active without the benefits.

## What is dated

The specific numerical tests – particular requirements for debt ratios,
dividend history and pricing – were written for a different market and a
different era, and cannot be applied mechanically today. The accounting
examples are just as old.

It is also worth noting where Graham himself ended up. Late in life he said he
no longer recommended the thorough analytical work for most people, because
the market had become more efficient. A broad index fund, bought regularly
over a long time, would give most people a better result. That is worth
keeping in mind when reading a 500-page book on stock analysis.

*The content is a walk through what the book argues, not individual financial
advice or a recommendation to buy or sell anything.*
